Why most online calculators give you the wrong number

Most S Corp vs LLC tax calculators you'll find online make the same two mistakes: they ignore your state income tax entirely, and they forget to subtract the S-Corp overhead costs — payroll service, quarterly filings, and the extra CPA time required once you elect S-Corp status. Consequently, they dramatically overstate your savings and make the S-Corp election look attractive well before the math actually supports it. This calculator includes all four tax layers — self-employment tax, federal income tax, state income tax, and S-Corp overhead — so the number you see is your real net savings, not a best-case estimate.

How to get the most accurate result from this calculator

Enter your net profit — revenue minus all business expenses, not your gross revenue. Then use the salary slider to set a reasonable W-2 salary for your role. The IRS requires S-Corp owners to pay themselves a salary that's reasonable for their industry and hours worked; the LLC vs S Corp calculator defaults to $60,000, but your number should reflect what you'd pay someone else to do your job. Finally, select your state — the difference between a zero-income-tax state like Texas or Florida and a high-tax state like California or New York can swing the S-Corp breakeven point by $15,000 or more in net profit. How the S-Corp Tax Savings Actually Work

Understanding the mechanism behind S-Corp savings makes the calculator result far more meaningful. The savings don't come from lower income tax rates — your federal and state income taxes are actually very similar under both structures. Instead, they come entirely from reducing the base on which self-employment tax is calculated.

The self-employment tax problem every LLC owner faces

As an LLC owner, your entire net profit is subject to self-employment tax. At 15.3% on the first $184,500 of net earnings (2026) — composed of 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare — SE tax adds up fast. Additionally, net earnings above $200,000 are subject to an extra 0.9% Medicare surcharge. While you can deduct half of the SE tax you pay, you still pay it on every dollar of profit. At $100,000 in net profit, that's roughly $14,130 in SE tax alone — before a single dollar of income tax.

What the S-Corp election actually changes

When you elect S-Corp status, your business pays you a W-2 salary. You pay SE tax — or more precisely, payroll taxes — only on that salary. The remaining profit passes through as a distribution, which is not subject to self-employment tax. Consequently, if your net profit is $100,000 and your salary is $60,000, you pay SE tax only on $60,000 instead of $100,000. That difference — $40,000 taxed at 15.3% — produces a gross SE tax savings of approximately $5,652. After subtracting payroll and CPA overhead of roughly $1,800, your net savings are approximately $3,852 per year.

The S-Corp Breakeven Point — When Does It Make Sense?

Below a certain profit threshold, the annual cost of running payroll and maintaining additional compliance outweighs the SE tax savings. Understanding exactly where that breakeven point sits — and what factors shift it — is, therefore, the most important part of the LLC vs S-Corp decision.

Why $50,000 is the commonly cited threshold

The $50,000 net profit figure you'll see in most guides is a reasonable starting point, but it's a simplified approximation. The actual breakeven depends on three variables: your net profit, your salary, and your S-Corp overhead cost. At $50,000 in net profit with a $40,000 salary, the gross SE tax savings are approximately $1,530. After $1,800 in overhead, you're actually slightly negative — the LLC is marginally more efficient. The breakeven at that overhead cost is closer to $55,000–$60,000 in net profit, not $50,000. Furthermore, in high-tax states like California — where the state also imposes minimum fees on S-Corps — the breakeven can push closer to $70,000+.

LLC vs S-Corp Calculator — Frequently Asked Questions

1 How does this calculator handle state income tax?
The calculator factors in state-specific income tax rates and S-Corp entity taxes (such as California's 1.5% S-Corp fee or minimum $800 tax), providing a far more realistic estimate than generic federal-only calculators.
2 What is considered a "reasonable salary" by the IRS?
A reasonable salary is what you would have to pay an outside employee to perform your exact job duties in your industry and geographic region. Setting your salary too low to avoid payroll taxes can lead to IRS audits and back penalties.
3 When is the deadline to file IRS Form 2553 for S-Corp status?
For existing LLCs, Form 2553 must be filed no later than 2 months and 15 days after the beginning of the tax year (March 15 for calendar-year taxpayers) for the election to take effect that tax year. Late election relief is often available under Rev. Proc. 2013-30.
