Why S-Corp Savings Calculations Can Be Misleading

Online S-Corp vs LLC calculators can produce very different results because they may use different assumptions about reasonable compensation, employment taxes, state taxes, deductions, and S-Corp operating costs.

A useful comparison should account for more than the potential employment-tax difference. Payroll processing, tax preparation, state-level taxes or fees, and other administrative costs can reduce the potential benefit of an S-Corp election.

This calculator therefore provides an estimated net comparison using the assumptions you enter. It should not be interpreted as an exact prediction of your final federal or state tax liability, because a complete tax calculation can depend on circumstances that are not captured by a simple calculator.

How the S-Corp Tax Savings Actually Work

Understanding the mechanism behind S-Corp savings makes the calculator result more meaningful. The potential benefit generally comes from the difference between self-employment tax under default pass-through taxation and payroll taxes on reasonable S-Corp wages. It is not simply a lower federal income-tax rate.

The self-employment tax calculation for an LLC

For a typical single-member LLC taxed by default as a disregarded entity, net earnings from self-employment are generally subject to self-employment tax. The calculation generally starts with 92.35% of net earnings, rather than applying 15.3% directly to every dollar of business profit. For 2026, the Social Security portion is subject to a $184,500 wage base, while the 2.9% Medicare portion has no wage-base limit.

A 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax can also apply to wages and self-employment income above thresholds that depend on filing status: $200,000 for single and certain other taxpayers, $250,000 for married filing jointly, and $125,000 for married filing separately. Other wages, self-employment earnings, and applicable tax rules can affect the calculation.

For example, with $100,000 of net self-employment earnings and no other factors affecting the calculation, the basic calculation is approximately:

  • $100,000 × 92.35% = $92,350 of net earnings subject to self-employment tax.
  • Social Security and Medicare taxes then apply under the applicable 2026 rules.
  • The resulting basic self-employment tax is approximately $14,130.

The actual tax calculation can differ when other wages, self-employment income, deductions, or Additional Medicare Tax apply.

What the S-Corp election actually changes

An eligible LLC that elects S-Corporation tax treatment generally pays its shareholder-employees through payroll. Compensation for services is treated as wages and is subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes. Remaining business income may generally pass through to the shareholder as a distribution and is not subject to self-employment tax, although the IRS can reclassify distributions as wages when they represent compensation for services.

For example, suppose the business has $100,000 of profit before owner compensation and payroll-tax effects and the owner receives a $60,000 salary. A simplified comparison is shown below.

The S-Corp Breakeven Point — When Does It Make Sense?

There is no universal S-Corp breakeven point such as $50,000, $60,000, or $80,000 of profit.

The point at which an election may produce a net benefit depends on the owner's reasonable compensation, payroll and accounting costs, state tax treatment, other income, deductions, and the amount of profit remaining after compensation and business expenses.

Why $50,000 is only a rough rule of thumb

You may see $50,000 or another profit figure described as the point at which an S-Corp becomes worthwhile. That figure is a simplified rule of thumb, not an IRS threshold.

For example, if a business earns $50,000 of net profit and the owner must receive a substantial portion as reasonable compensation, the potential employment-tax difference may be relatively small. Payroll, tax-preparation, state-level S-Corp costs, and other administrative expenses can reduce or eliminate the potential benefit.

At higher profit levels, the potential difference can become larger, but the result still depends on the salary required for the owner's actual work. Use the calculator to test the assumptions rather than relying on a single profit threshold.

How to Set a Reasonable S-Corp Salary — The IRS Standard

Your S-Corp salary is an important input in the LLC vs. S-Corp calculation. However, the IRS does not establish a universal salary percentage or a fixed minimum salary for every shareholder-employee.

What "reasonable compensation" actually means

The IRS says the key question is what the shareholder-employee actually does for the S-Corporation and how the corporation generates its gross receipts. Relevant factors include training and experience, duties and responsibilities, time and effort, comparable compensation, compensation agreements, dividend history, and payments made to non-shareholder employees.

The IRS may reclassify distributions as wages when they represent compensation for services. The case David E. Watson, P.C. v. United States is one example of the courts supporting the government's ability to challenge compensation treatment.

Rather than selecting the lowest salary that produces the largest calculator result, use compensation data that reflects the actual role and document the reasoning behind the amount.

Salary benchmarks by profession

There is no IRS-approved salary range for each profession. The appropriate comparison depends on the actual services performed, location, experience, hours, responsibilities, and business circumstances.

A published occupational wage does not automatically establish reasonable compensation for a particular shareholder-employee. Before filing your S-Corp return, document the duties performed, time devoted to the business, relevant market data, and other factors supporting the salary. How Your State Changes the LLC vs S-Corp Math

Federal employment-tax rules provide the starting point for an LLC vs. S-Corp comparison, but state tax and compliance rules can materially change the final result. States differ in how they tax LLCs and S-Corporations, whether they impose franchise or excise taxes, and what filing or minimum-tax requirements apply.

A federal-only calculation should therefore be treated as a starting point rather than the final answer for every state.

State factors that can change the calculation

  • Individual income tax: A state's individual income-tax rates can affect the owner's overall tax liability.
  • S-Corp-level taxes: Some states impose entity-level franchise, excise, or other taxes on S-Corporations.
  • Minimum taxes or fees: Certain states impose minimum annual taxes or filing fees regardless of profitability.
  • LLC-specific taxes: Some states also impose annual LLC taxes, fees, or other charges.
  • Local taxes: Cities and other local jurisdictions can impose additional business or income taxes in some circumstances.
  • Different treatment of S-Corporation income: State conformity with federal S-Corp rules is not uniform.

For example, California imposes an $800 annual minimum franchise tax on many corporations and LLCs, while its S-Corporations can also be subject to a separate 1.5% tax on net income. The applicable rules should be checked for the specific entity and tax year rather than assuming that a federal S-Corp calculation applies unchanged.

Likewise, a business operating in New York City may encounter city-level corporate taxes and other rules that require a separate state and local analysis.

ℹ️ Important: States without a broad individual income tax are not automatically identical for S-Corp purposes. Franchise taxes, excise taxes, filing fees, and other state requirements can still affect the calculation. A federal-only result should therefore be treated as a starting point rather than a complete state-specific estimate.

How to Make the S-Corp Election — Step by Step

An LLC that is eligible to elect S-Corporation tax treatment generally makes the federal election by filing Form 2553 with the IRS. The election does not create a new state-law entity; it changes the federal tax classification of an eligible entity.

Path 1 — LLC electing S-Corp tax status

1
Confirm S-Corp eligibility for your LLC An eligible entity generally must meet the S-Corporation requirements, including having no more than 100 shareholders, only allowable shareholders, and one class of stock. Nonresident aliens generally cannot be shareholders. Certain corporations are also ineligible.
2
File Form 2553 with the IRS Form 2553 is used to elect S-Corporation treatment. For a calendar-year taxpayer, the normal deadline is generally 2 months and 15 days after the beginning of the tax year for which the election is to take effect, or during the preceding tax year. The IRS provides procedures for certain late-election situations when applicable.
3
Set up payroll and establish reasonable compensation A shareholder who provides services to an S-Corporation generally must receive reasonable compensation before non-wage distributions are made. Payroll involves withholding and employer payroll-tax responsibilities.
4
File Form 1120-S annually An S-Corporation generally files Form 1120-S and provides shareholders with Schedule K-1 information for their share of the corporation's income, deductions, credits, and other items. Form 1120-S is generally due on the 15th day of the third month after the corporation's tax year ends.
5
Review state and business records The federal S-Corp election does not eliminate state-level filing, tax, payroll, or business requirements. Review the applicable state rules and update business records where necessary. Whether an operating agreement, organizational documents, bank records, or other registrations need to be changed depends on the entity and state. See our operating agreement guide for additional information.

Path 2 — Form a corporation and elect S-Corp treatment

A new corporation that meets the federal requirements can elect S-Corporation treatment by filing Form 2553. An eligible LLC can also elect to be treated as an S-Corporation for federal tax purposes. The appropriate route depends on the entity being formed, ownership structure, state law, and tax objectives.

Professional formation or tax services may offer assistance with these steps, but their pricing, included services, and eligibility requirements should be reviewed before purchase.

Salary, overhead, and how the election works

How do I determine my reasonable S-Corp salary?

The IRS does not prescribe a universal salary percentage, such as 40%–60% of profit, or a fixed salary range for each profession.

For a shareholder who provides services to an S-Corporation, reasonable compensation depends on the actual services performed and the facts and circumstances of the business. Relevant considerations can include training and experience, duties and responsibilities, time and effort, comparable compensation, compensation agreements, and how the corporation's gross receipts are generated.

Salary surveys and Bureau of Labor Statistics data can be useful when researching comparable compensation, but they are not IRS-approved salary tables or automatic safe harbors.

Keeping records explaining how compensation was determined can be useful for demonstrating the basis for the amount. If you are uncertain about reasonable compensation, consider discussing the calculation with a qualified tax professional.

Can a single-member LLC elect S-Corp status?

Yes. A qualifying single-member LLC can generally elect S-Corporation tax treatment by filing IRS Form 2553.

The LLC remains an LLC under applicable state law; the election changes its federal tax classification. Once the election applies, an owner who performs services for the business generally must receive reasonable compensation through payroll, and the S-Corporation generally files Form 1120-S annually.

This is commonly described as an LLC taxed as an S-Corporation. It is important to distinguish the federal tax election from creating a corporation under state law.

S-Corp overhead costs — what to expect

What does S-Corp overhead actually cost per year?

There is no universal annual S-Corp overhead amount. Costs can include:

  • Payroll software or payroll-provider fees
  • Additional accounting and tax-preparation work
  • Payroll-tax filing and administration
  • State franchise, excise, or other entity-level taxes
  • State filing and compliance fees
  • Professional advice concerning compensation or the election

If this calculator uses $1,800 per year as its default overhead assumption, that is a modeling assumption rather than a standard S-Corp cost.

Your actual additional cost could be lower or higher depending on your payroll setup, accountant, state, number of employees, and the services you purchase. You can adjust the calculator's overhead assumption to see how the estimated result changes.

Timing, deadlines, and reversing the election

When is the deadline to file Form 2553 for an S-Corp election?

The normal federal deadline depends on when you want the election to take effect.

For a calendar-year taxpayer, Form 2553 is generally due no later than 2 months and 15 days after the beginning of the tax year for which the election is intended to apply. An eligible new entity generally has the same basic timing rule based on when it begins operating or when the election is intended to take effect.

The IRS provides specific rules for late-election relief in qualifying circumstances. Because the filing deadline can depend on the entity's tax year and circumstances, use the current IRS Form 2553 instructions rather than assuming that March 15 or December 31 applies in every situation.

Reversing the election and liability protection

Can I reverse an S-Corp election if it no longer makes sense?

An S-Corporation election can generally be voluntarily revoked, but the process has specific requirements.

A revocation generally requires a written statement filed with the IRS and, in many circumstances, the consent of shareholders owning more than 50% of the corporation's shares. The effective date depends on when the revocation is filed and the date specified in the statement.

There can also be restrictions on making another S-Corporation election after a voluntary termination. A five-year limitation generally applies, although exceptions can apply in certain circumstances and the IRS can provide consent in qualifying cases.

Because revoking an election can have significant tax consequences, evaluate the longer-term effect before making or terminating the election.

Does the S-Corp election affect my LLC's liability protection?

An S-Corp election is generally a federal tax classification election, not a conversion of an LLC into a corporation under state law.
