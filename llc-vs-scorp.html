Consequently, they dramatically overstate your savings and make the S-Corp election look attractive well before the math actually supports it. How the S-Corp Tax Savings Actually Work

Understanding the mechanism behind S-Corp savings makes the calculator result far more meaningful. The savings don't come from lower income tax rates — your federal and state income taxes are actually very similar under both structures. Instead, they come entirely from reducing the base on which self-employment tax is calculated.

The self-employment tax problem every LLC owner faces

As an LLC owner, your entire net profit is subject to self-employment tax. At 15.3% on the first $184,500 of net earnings (2026) — composed of 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare — SE tax adds up fast. Additionally, net earnings above $200,000 are subject to an extra 0.9% Medicare surcharge. While you can deduct half of the SE tax you pay, you still pay it on every dollar of profit. At $100,000 in net profit, that's roughly $14,130 in SE tax alone — before a single dollar of income tax.

What the S-Corp election actually changes

When you elect S-Corp status, your business pays you a W-2 salary. You pay SE tax — or more precisely, payroll taxes — only on that salary. The remaining profit passes through as a distribution, which is not subject to self-employment tax. Consequently, if your net profit is $100,000 and your salary is $60,000, you pay SE tax only on $60,000 instead of $100,000. That difference — $40,000 taxed at 15.3% — produces a gross SE tax savings of approximately $5,652. After subtracting payroll and CPA overhead of roughly $1,800, your net savings are approximately $3,852 per year.

The S-Corp Breakeven Point — When Does It Make Sense?

Below a certain profit threshold, the annual cost of running payroll and maintaining additional compliance outweighs the SE tax savings. Understanding exactly where that breakeven point sits — and what factors shift it — is, therefore, the most important part of the LLC vs S-Corp decision.

Why $50,000 is the commonly cited threshold

The $50,000 net profit figure you'll see in most guides is a reasonable starting point, but it's a simplified approximation. The actual breakeven depends on three variables: your net profit, your salary, and your S-Corp overhead cost. At $50,000 in net profit with a $40,000 salary, the gross SE tax savings are approximately $1,530. After $1,800 in overhead, you're actually slightly negative — the LLC is marginally more efficient. The breakeven at that overhead cost is closer to $55,000–$60,000 in net profit, not $50,000. Furthermore, in high-tax states like California — where the state also imposes minimum fees on S-Corps — the breakeven can push closer to $80,000.

How to Set a Reasonable S-Corp Salary — The IRS Standard

Your S-Corp salary is the most consequential variable in the LLC vs S-Corp calculator — and it's the one the IRS pays the most attention to. Setting it too low is the primary audit risk with S-Corp status. Chasing maximum savings by setting it too high, however, unnecessarily shrinks your net benefit. Understanding the IRS standard gives you a defensible number that maximizes savings without creating compliance exposure.

What "reasonable compensation" actually means

The IRS defines reasonable compensation as the salary you would pay a non-owner employee to perform the same services. Specifically, it considers your industry, the nature of your work, your hours, your comparable market rate, and your company's revenue. There is no fixed formula or safe harbor — the IRS evaluates it based on facts and circumstances. However, several court cases have established practical benchmarks: in Watson v. Commissioner (2012), the Tax Court ruled that a CPA earning $200,000+ in distributions while paying himself $24,000 in salary was clearly unreasonable. Consequently, most tax professionals recommend setting your salary between 40% and 60% of net profit as a starting point, then validating it against salary surveys for your role.

How Your State Changes the LLC vs S-Corp Math

Federal SE tax savings are the same regardless of where you live. However, your state's treatment of LLCs and S-Corps can significantly shift the net savings in either direction — sometimes making the S-Corp election clearly superior, and occasionally making it worse than staying as an LLC.

States where S-Corp savings are highest

States with high income tax rates but no additional S-Corp-specific fees benefit the most from the S-Corp structure. In California, the 9.3% state income tax on middle-bracket income means that every dollar shifted from SE income to distribution also saves state tax, amplifying the federal savings. Similarly, Oregon (9.9%), Minnesota (9.85%), and New Jersey (6.37%) all produce meaningful additional savings on top of the federal SE tax reduction.

States where the S-Corp math is less favorable

While California's state income tax amplifies federal S-Corp savings, the state also imposes an $800 minimum franchise tax on S-Corps and LLCs alike, plus a 1.5% S-Corp net income tax on top of personal income tax. Consequently, the net California S-Corp advantage is significantly lower than the federal calculation alone suggests. New York City similarly imposes a general corporation tax on S-Corps that doesn't apply to LLCs, making the LLC more attractive for NYC-based business owners at certain income levels.

ℹ️ Zero-income-tax states: the math is simpler: In states with no income tax — Texas, Florida, Nevada, Washington, Wyoming, South Dakota, and Alaska — the S-Corp savings calculation is purely federal. There are no additional state-level complications, no minimum S-Corp fees beyond the standard LLC annual report, and no state-level income tax amplification. In these states, the breakeven point is typically $55,000–$65,000 in net profit.

The S-Corp Costs Most Calculators Ignore

Gross SE tax savings are not the same as net savings — yet most S Corp vs LLC calculators treat them as identical. In reality, electing S-Corp status adds real, recurring annual costs that must be netted against the savings before the comparison is honest.

How to reduce S-Corp overhead costs

Overhead is not fixed — there's meaningful room to optimize it. Specifically, using a modern payroll service like Gusto or Wave Payroll at $40–$75/month instead of a full-service payroll bureau cuts costs by $300–$600/year. Additionally, if your CPA already handles your LLC taxes, the marginal cost of upgrading to Form 1120-S is typically $300–$600 rather than a full additional engagement. Therefore, business owners who are already working with a CPA and use a lean payroll tool can often bring total S-Corp overhead below $1,200/year, improving the net savings materially.

How to Make the S-Corp Election — Step by Step

If the calculator confirms that S-Corp status is worth it for your situation, the actual election process is straightforward. There are two paths depending on whether you already have an LLC or are forming a new entity.

Path 1 — LLC electing S-Corp tax status (most common)

1
Confirm S-Corp eligibility for your LLC Your LLC must have fewer than 100 members, all members must be U.S. citizens or permanent residents, and your LLC must have a single class of membership interest. Additionally, certain types of businesses — financial institutions, insurance companies, and international sales corporations — are ineligible for S-Corp status.
2
File Form 2553 with the IRS Form 2553 is the Election by a Small Business Corporation form. It's free to file and can be submitted online, by mail, or by fax. To apply the election to the current tax year, you must file by March 15 of that year (or within 75 days of formation for a new entity). For the following tax year, you can file at any point before December 31.
3
Set up payroll before your first paycheck Once the S-Corp election is active, you must pay yourself a W-2 salary — not just take distributions. Set up a payroll service like Gusto or QuickBooks Payroll, establish your salary amount, and begin running payroll. Payroll taxes (Form 941) must be deposited quarterly at a minimum.
4
File Form 1120-S annually instead of Schedule C S-Corps file a separate corporate tax return — Form 1120-S — in addition to your personal return. Your CPA will prepare a Schedule K-1 that flows your share of S-Corp income to your personal Form 1040. This is the main reason S-Corp accounting costs more than LLC accounting.
5
Update your operating agreement and bank account Notify your bank of the S-Corp election, update your operating agreement or corporate bylaws to reflect the tax status change, and confirm that your registered agent is aware of the new filing obligations. See our operating agreement guide for required provisions.

Path 2 — Form a new S-Corp or convert from scratch

If you're starting fresh, you can form a corporation (not an LLC) and simultaneously elect S-Corp status by filing Form 2553 within 75 days of incorporation. Alternatively, you can form an LLC and make the S-Corp election at the same time. Either way, the most efficient path for most small business owners is a formation service that handles both steps together. Frequently Asked Questions — LLC vs S-Corp Tax Calculator

How accurate is this LLC vs S-Corp tax calculator?

This calculator uses official 2026 IRS tax brackets, self-employment tax rates (15.3%), Medicare surcharges, state income tax rates, QBI deduction models, and realistic administrative overhead estimates. While it provides a highly accurate diagnostic estimate, actual tax liabilities depend on itemized deductions, local taxes, retirement contributions, and CPA guidance.

What net profit level should I consider S-Corp status?

Most businesses begin seeing meaningful net savings above $50,000 to $60,000 in net profit. Below $40,000 in net profit, S-Corp payroll setup and tax return preparation costs usually exceed self-employment tax savings.

Salary, overhead, and how the election works

How do I determine my reasonable S-Corp salary?

The IRS requires S-Corp owner-employees to pay themselves a "reasonable salary" based on market rates for similar work. Benchmarks can be obtained from Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) data, industry salary surveys, or guidance from your CPA.

Can a single-member LLC elect S-Corp status?

Yes. Single-member LLCs are eligible to elect S-Corp tax status with the IRS by submitting Form 2553. The legal entity remains a single-member LLC under state law while being taxed as an S-Corporation federally.

S-Corp overhead costs — what to expect

What does S-Corp overhead actually cost per year?

Typical annual S-Corp overhead includes automated payroll software ($500–$900/yr), Form 1120-S corporate tax return preparation by a CPA ($500–$1,500/yr), and state corporate franchise fees if applicable ($0–$800/yr). Average total overhead is roughly $1,800/year.

Timing, deadlines, and reversing the election

When is the deadline to file Form 2553 for S-Corp election?

To take effect in the current tax year, IRS Form 2553 must be filed no later than 2 months and 15 days after the beginning of the tax year (March 15th for calendar-year taxpayers). Elections filed later take effect the following tax year unless relief for late election is granted by the IRS.

Reversing the election and liability protection

Can I reverse an S-Corp election if it no longer makes sense?

Yes. You can revoke an S-Corp tax election by submitting a statement of revocation to the IRS. Once revoked, the LLC reverts back to standard pass-through taxation (sole proprietorship or partnership).

Does the S-Corp election affect my LLC's liability protection?

No. Electing S-Corp status is strictly a federal tax classification. Your underlying entity remains a state-level LLC, preserving all personal asset protection, limited liability status, and corporate shield benefits provided under state statute.
