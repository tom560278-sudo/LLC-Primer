What is an LLC?

The Business Structure That Protects Your Assets Without the Corporate Headache

A limited liability company (LLC) is a legal business entity created under state law that separates your personal assets from your business debts and lawsuits. In simple terms, what is an LLC? It's the structure that gives you the protection of a corporation with the simplicity of a sole proprietorship — and it's why over 35% of U.S. businesses operate as LLCs today.

Specifically, an LLC protects your home, personal savings, and personal vehicles from being seized to pay business debts or satisfy a lawsuit judgment. Without that protection, moreover, a single client dispute or business debt could threaten everything you own personally. This guide is part of our LLC Guide and covers exactly how an LLC works, the 6 types, how it's taxed, what it costs, and whether you need one.

How an LLC Works — The Core Mechanics

A Separate Legal Entity From Its Owners

The moment you file Articles of Organization with your state's Secretary of State, your LLC becomes a separate legal entity. Consequently, it can own property, enter contracts, open bank accounts, and be sued — all independently of you as an individual. This separation is the foundation of everything the LLC does. Specifically, your personal assets exist on one side of a legal wall, and your business assets exist on the other.

Furthermore, unlike a sole proprietorship — where you and the business are legally the same person — an LLC means a creditor suing your business can only reach what the LLC owns. Your personal home, savings, and vehicle remain on the protected side of that wall. As a result, this structure is why the LLC has become the default choice for freelancers, small business owners, real estate investors, and e-commerce sellers across the U.S.

Many sole proprietors register a DBA (Doing Business As) to operate under a brand name — but a DBA doesn't create a separate legal entity and provides no liability protection. An LLC is a fundamentally different structure.

What an LLC Actually Protects — And What It Doesn't

The Three Asset Categories an LLC Shields

Personal asset protection is the primary reason most people form an LLC. Specifically, when a creditor wins a judgment against your LLC, they can only go after what the LLC owns — not what you own personally. The three categories of personal assets that an LLC shields are your financial accounts (personal bank accounts, investment portfolios), your real estate (personal home and property), and your personal property (vehicles, belongings, and other personal assets).

In practice, this means a client lawsuit, a vendor dispute, or a business bankruptcy cannot touch your personal savings. Furthermore, a landlord or supplier you owe money to cannot put a lien on your home. That protection is real, legally enforceable in every U.S. state, and worth significantly more than the $132 average formation cost.

Four Situations Where LLC Protection Can Fail

However, the protection is conditional — not absolute. Courts can "pierce the corporate veil" and hold you personally liable if you don't maintain the LLC correctly. The most common failure modes are:

⚠️ Four Things That Destroy LLC Protection
  1. 1. Commingling funds — Using the same bank account for personal and business transactions removes the legal separation the LLC depends on.
  2. 2. Personal guarantees — Signing personally on a business loan or lease makes you personally liable for that debt, regardless of the LLC structure.
  3. 3. Administrative dissolution — Failing to file annual reports or pay state fees causes your state to dissolve the LLC. A dissolved LLC has no liability protection.
  4. 4. Fraud or personal negligence — Courts disregard LLC protection entirely when the member personally commits fraud or directly causes harm to a third party.

Consequently, the operating agreement, separate bank account, and annual compliance are not optional extras — they're what makes the protection real. See our guide on creating an operating agreement to set this up correctly from day one.

Single-Member vs Multi-Member: The Key Difference

The most important structural choice is whether your company has one owner or multiple owners. A single-member LLC is treated by the IRS as a "disregarded entity" — meaning the IRS ignores the entity and taxes you directly as if you were a sole proprietor. You report income on Schedule C of your personal Form 1040. By contrast, a multi-member LLC is treated as a partnership by default. It files Form 1065 with the IRS (deadline: March 15 annually), and each member receives a Schedule K-1 showing their share of income to report on their personal return.

💡 For most first-time business owners: a single-member structure is the fastest, simplest, and cheapest option. You can always add members later by amending your operating agreement.

PLLC: For Licensed Professionals

Professional LLC (PLLC) is required in many states for licensed professionals — including doctors, lawyers, architects, engineers, and accountants. Specifically, this structure works the same as a standard company, but it adds licensing board approval requirements during formation. Additionally, PLLC members are protected from each other's malpractice — but each member remains personally liable for their own professional errors.

California, notably, does not recognize PLLCs and requires licensed professionals to form Professional Corporations instead.

Series LLC: For Multiple Assets or Properties

A Series LLC allows one parent entity to create separate "series" — each with its own members, assets, and liabilities — under a single formation filing. As a result, real estate investors who own multiple properties can shield each property from the liabilities of others without forming a separate entity for each one. This structure is currently available in 22 states, including Texas, Delaware, Illinois, and Nevada. However, it's complex to maintain and requires strict financial separation between each series to preserve the protection.

📊 Which Type Is Most Common?

The single-member structure is by far the most common in the U.S. According to the IRS, limited liability companies now represent 71.7% of all partnerships filed — and the majority of those are single-owner structures. If you're starting a business alone, a single-member LLC is almost certainly the right starting point.

How an LLC Is Taxed — All 4 Options

The Entity Has No Tax Classification of Its Own

This surprises many new business owners: the IRS does not have a specific tax category for limited liability companies. Consequently, the tax treatment depends entirely on how many members it has — and whether it makes a tax election. Default Tax: Pass-Through and Self-Employment Tax

By default, profits from your limited liability company pass directly to your personal tax return. The entity, therefore, pays no federal income tax at the entity level. Instead, you pay income tax at your personal bracket plus 15.3% self-employment tax on all net profit. Specifically, that 15.3% breaks down as 12.4% Social Security (on income up to $176,100 in 2026) and 2.9% Medicare (no cap). On $80,000 in net profit, consequently, that's $12,240 in SE tax alone — before income tax. One question that comes up immediately: once the money is in the LLC, what is the correct way to transfer it to yourself — and does it affect how much SE tax you owe? The answer depends entirely on your LLC structure and tax election.

⚠️ The Self-Employment Tax Reality

Self-employment tax hits every dollar of profit by default. At $100,000 net profit, you owe $15,300 in SE tax — money that pays no rent, hires no employees, and grows no business. That's why the S-corp election becomes worth considering once profits consistently exceed $60,000–$80,000/year. See the full breakdown in our LLC tax benefits guide.

The S-Corp Election: Reducing SE Tax Above $60K

A limited liability company can file Form 2553 to elect S-corporation tax treatment. As a result, instead of paying SE tax on all profit, you split income: you pay yourself a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes) and take the rest as distributions — which are not subject to SE tax. At $100,000 profit with a $60,000 salary, for example, you save approximately $6,120 in SE tax annually. For the full comparison, see our LLC vs S-Corp guide.

How to Form an LLC — 5 Steps

The Formation Process from Start to Approval

Forming an LLC is simpler than most people expect. In most states, moreover, you can complete the entire process online in under an hour. Here are the five steps, in the order you need to complete them:

1
Choose your state and check name availability
Form your LLC in the state where you live and operate. Then, specifically, search your state's Secretary of State business name database to confirm your chosen name is available. Your name must include "LLC," "L.L.C.," or "Limited Liability Company" — and it must be distinguishable from other registered businesses in that state.
2
Appoint a registered agent
Every LLC is required to have a registered agent in its formation state. Your registered agent receives official legal documents — such as lawsuits and state notices — on the LLC's behalf. You can be your own agent if you have a physical address in the state, or use a professional service for $39–$125/year for added privacy — see our best registered agent services comparison, or go directly with Northwest ($39/year), our top pick.
3
File Articles of Organization and pay the state fee
Submit your Articles of Organization through your state's Secretary of State website. Pay the filing fee ($35–$500, average $132). Processing time ranges from same-day in some states to 2–3 weeks in others. Expedited processing is available in most states for an additional fee.
4
Create an operating agreement
Draft an operating agreement that defines ownership percentages, management structure, profit distribution, and decision-making rules. Furthermore, even if your state doesn't require it, this document is critical for maintaining the legal separation that makes LLC protection enforceable. Free templates exist, but professional review is worthwhile for multi-member LLCs.
5
Get your EIN and open a business bank account
Apply for a free Employer Identification Number (EIN) at IRS.gov — takes 10 minutes, instantly issued online. Then, immediately, open a dedicated business bank account in your LLC's name. All business revenue and expenses must flow through this account — never your personal one. This single step is what makes the liability protection real in court.
🛠️ Do It Yourself or Use a Service?

You can form an LLC yourself directly through your state's website — no service required. However, if you want to save time or want your personal address kept off public records, a formation service handles the paperwork and often includes registered agent service. Do You Actually Need an LLC?

The Income and Risk Threshold That Matters

Not every business needs an LLC immediately. However, most businesses need one sooner than their owners think. The two factors that determine whether you need an LLC right now are your liability exposure and your revenue level. Specifically, if you have any of the following, forming an LLC should be a priority — not a "someday" task:

Form an LLC If Any of These Apply
🟢
You have personal assets worth protecting
A home, savings, investments, or a vehicle. Without an LLC, a business lawsuit can reach all of these directly.
🟢
You work directly with clients or customers
Service businesses, e-commerce, contractors, and consultants all face liability from work quality, product defects, or client disputes.
🟢
You're hiring employees or contractors
Employment-related claims — wrongful termination, workplace injuries, discrimination — expose you personally without an LLC.
🟢
Your annual revenue exceeds $30,000
At this level, the LLC's annual cost ($150–$400/year) is less than 1.2% of revenue — and the protection it provides is worth significantly more.
🟢
You want business credibility and a dedicated bank account
Clients, banks, and vendors treat an LLC differently than an individual. Additionally, an LLC name on invoices and contracts signals legitimacy.

When You Can Wait to Form an LLC

That said, not everyone needs an LLC immediately. Specifically, if you're testing a business idea with under $10,000 in revenue, no client liability risk, and no personal assets to protect — a sole proprietorship is a reasonable starting point. Nevertheless, once the business becomes real and recurring income starts, the cost of forming an LLC is trivially small compared to the exposure of operating without one.

The U.S. Small Business Administration also recommends evaluating your liability exposure before choosing a structure.

BOTTOM LINE: WHAT IS AN LLC?
An LLC is the legal structure that separates your personal assets from your business risks — formed in your state, owned by members, taxed by default as a pass-through entity, and maintained through simple annual compliance. It's the most popular business structure in the U.S. for a reason: it solves the three biggest problems small business owners face — personal liability, tax flexibility, and operational simplicity — at a cost most businesses can afford from day one.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Frequently Asked Questions

Understanding What an LLC Is

01 What does LLC stand for?

LLC stands for Limited Liability Company. It is a legal business structure created under state law that combines the personal asset protection of a corporation with the pass-through tax treatment and operational flexibility of a sole proprietorship or partnership.

02 Is an LLC a corporation?

No, an LLC is not a corporation. While both entity types provide personal liability protection for their owners, an LLC has much simpler governance (members instead of shareholders and boards of directors), fewer formal meeting requirements, and pass-through taxation by default.

03 What is the difference between an LLC and a sole proprietorship?

A sole proprietorship offers zero legal separation between you and your business, meaning personal savings, vehicles, and real estate can be seized to satisfy business debts or lawsuits. An LLC creates a separate legal entity that shields your personal assets from business liabilities.

LLC Taxes and Costs

04 Does an LLC pay taxes?

By default, an LLC is a pass-through entity that pays no federal income tax at the entity level. Business profits pass directly to the owners' personal tax returns. However, owners pay income tax plus 15.3% self-employment tax on net profits unless an S-corp tax election is made.

05 How much does it cost to start an LLC?

Government state filing fees range from $35 (Montana) to $500 (Massachusetts), with a 50-state average of $132 in 2026. Ongoing annual report costs range from $0 to $300/year depending on your state of formation.

06 Can a single person own an LLC?

Yes! A Single-Member LLC (SMLLC) is owned by one individual or entity. It is the most common business structure in the United States, offering full liability protection with minimal administrative paperwork.

LLC Formation and Compliance

07 How long does it take to form an LLC?

Online state processing times range from immediate or same-day (in states like Ohio, Colorado, or Nevada) to 2–3 weeks for paper mail filings. Most states offer 24-hour expedited processing for a small government fee.

08 Do I need an operating agreement for an LLC?

Yes. Even in states where an operating agreement is not required to be filed with the Secretary of State, drafting one is essential to prove personal asset separation in court, open business bank accounts, and set owner rights.

09 What happens to an LLC when the owner dies?

For a single-member LLC, ownership passes to the owner's estate or designated beneficiaries according to their will or state probate law. For multi-member LLCs, the operating agreement typically defines buy-sell provisions or transfer rules upon a member's death.
