What is an LLC?

A Flexible Business Structure With Limited Liability Protection

A limited liability company (LLC) is a business entity created under state law. It generally separates the LLC's legal obligations from the personal assets of its members, which can help protect personal property from business debts and certain claims against the company. That protection is not absolute, however, and personal liability can still arise in situations such as personal guarantees, certain taxes, fraud, or failure to maintain appropriate separation between the business and its owners.

In simple terms, what is an LLC? An LLC combines flexible management and ownership rules with liability protection, while offering several federal tax-classification options. For federal income-tax purposes, a domestic single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity by default, while a domestic LLC with two or more members is generally treated as a partnership unless it elects corporate treatment. An eligible LLC can also elect to be taxed as an S corporation.

How an LLC Works — The Core Mechanics

A Separate Legal Entity From Its Owners

An LLC is a legal entity created under state law when its formation documents are properly filed and the LLC becomes effective under the state's rules. Once formed, an LLC can generally own property, enter contracts, open business bank accounts, and bring or defend legal actions in its own name.

This separation is a key part of an LLC's liability structure. The LLC's business assets and obligations are generally treated separately from the personal assets and obligations of its members. However, limited liability is not absolute. Personal guarantees, certain taxes, personal wrongdoing, and other circumstances can result in personal liability.

Unlike a sole proprietorship, where the owner and business are generally not separate legal entities, an LLC provides a formal legal structure between the business and its owners. Maintaining appropriate separation between personal and business finances and following applicable state requirements can help preserve that distinction.

Many sole proprietors use a DBA (Doing Business As) to operate under a business or brand name. A DBA can provide a different operating name, but it does not by itself create a separate legal entity or provide limited liability protection. An LLC is a different legal structure with its own rights, obligations, and liability framework.

What You Need to Form an LLC

Forming an LLC involves several core requirements, although the exact rules, documents, and fees vary by state. Most states require a compliant business name, a registered agent, formation documents, and a state filing fee. An operating agreement is also useful for documenting how the LLC will be owned and managed, even when state law does not require one.

REQUIREMENT DETAILS & DESCRIPTION
🟢 A Compliant Business Name Your LLC name must follow your state's naming rules and generally include an LLC designator such as “LLC,” “L.L.C.,” or “Limited Liability Company.” The name must also meet the state's availability or distinguishability requirements.
🟢 Articles of Organization The formation document used to create the LLC under state law. Some states use a different name, such as Certificate of Formation or Certificate of Organization. Required information varies by state.
🟢 A Registered Agent A person or business designated to receive official legal and state documents for the LLC. The registered agent must meet the state's address and availability requirements.
🟢 State Filing Fee The government fee charged to submit the LLC's formation filing. The amount varies by state and may be accompanied by additional required costs in some states.
🟠 Operating Agreement (Recommended) An internal document that can define ownership, management, voting, profit distributions, and other operating rules. Requirements vary by state, and it generally does not need to be filed with the state.
💡 After Formation: Two More Steps

Once your LLC is approved, get an EIN if applicable and open a dedicated business bank account. Keeping business and personal finances separate helps maintain clear financial records and supports the separation between you and your LLC. What an LLC Actually Protects — And What It Doesn't

The Three Asset Categories an LLC Can Help Protect

Personal asset protection is one of the main reasons people form an LLC. Generally, business creditors and claimants pursue assets owned by the LLC rather than the owner's separate personal assets. Personal assets may include financial accounts such as personal bank and investment accounts, real estate such as a personal home, and personal property such as vehicles and other belongings.

This protection is not absolute. Personal liability can still arise from personal guarantees, personal wrongdoing, certain taxes or obligations, or circumstances where the legal separation between the owner and LLC is not properly maintained.

Four Situations That Can Put LLC Protection at Risk

LLC liability protection depends on the circumstances and applicable state law. Common issues that can create personal exposure include:

⚠️ Four Things That Destroy LLC Protection
  1. 1. Commingling funds — Mixing personal and business finances can weaken the separation between the owner and LLC.
  2. 2. Personal Guarantees — Personally guaranteeing a business loan, lease, or other obligation can make you responsible for that debt.
  3. 3. Administrative Dissolution — Failing to maintain required filings or fees can cause an LLC to lose its active status and may affect its protections.
  4. 4. Personal Wrongdoing — An LLC generally does not protect an owner from liability for their own fraud, negligence, or other wrongful acts.

Keeping separate finances, maintaining required records, following state compliance requirements, and using appropriate business documents can help preserve the LLC's legal separation.

The 6 Types of LLCs Explained

Choosing the Right Structure for Your Situation

LLCs can be structured in different ways depending on ownership, management, professional licensing, and state law. Some categories describe who owns the LLC, while others describe how it is managed or whether special state rules apply. Understanding these distinctions can help you choose an appropriate structure before filing.

Important: These categories can overlap. For example, an LLC can be single-member and manager-managed, or multi-member and member-managed. State availability and requirements for PLLCs and Series LLCs vary, so check the rules in the state where you plan to form the business.

Single-Member vs Multi-Member: The Key Difference

The main structural difference is the number of owners. A single-member LLC is generally treated by the IRS as a disregarded entity for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. Its business income and expenses are generally reported on the owner's federal tax return, often using Schedule C for a business operated by an individual.

A multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. The LLC generally files Form 1065, and members typically receive Schedule K-1 showing their allocated share of the partnership's income, deductions, and other items. Filing deadlines can vary depending on the tax year and circumstances.

PLLC: For Licensed Professionals

A Professional Limited Liability Company (PLLC) is a state-law business structure available to certain licensed professionals in states that permit it. Requirements vary by state and profession and may include professional licensing, ownership restrictions, or approval from a licensing authority.

A PLLC can generally provide limited liability for business obligations, but it does not normally protect a professional from personal liability for their own malpractice or professional misconduct. Whether one professional can be liable for another member's professional acts also depends on applicable state law.

California, for example, does not provide a PLLC structure under its LLC law for licensed professional practices; certain professionals may instead need to use a professional corporation or another permitted entity structure.

Series LLC: For Multiple Assets or Properties

A Series LLC is a special LLC structure permitted by certain states that can allow a parent LLC to establish separate series with distinct assets, members, or business activities. Where recognized and properly maintained, each series may receive separate liability treatment under state law.

This structure can be useful for businesses managing multiple properties or separate lines of activity, but the rules are complex and vary significantly by state. Requirements may include separate records, accounting, assets, and other formalities for each series. Before using a Series LLC, review the rules of the state involved and consider professional legal and tax advice.

📊 Which LLC Structure Fits Your Business?

There is no single LLC structure that is appropriate for every business. A Single-member LLC may be a straightforward option for an individual owner, while a multi-member, manager-managed, professional, or Series LLC may make sense in different circumstances. Your ownership, business activities, state requirements, and tax objectives should guide the decision.

How an LLC Is Taxed — 4 Federal Tax Classifications

LLCs Can Have Different Federal Tax Classifications

The IRS does not treat an LLC as a separate federal income-tax classification by default. Instead, an LLC's federal tax treatment generally depends on the number of members and whether the LLC makes an election to be taxed as a corporation.

For federal tax purposes, an LLC may generally be treated as a disregarded entity, partnership, S corporation, or C corporation. The appropriate treatment depends on the LLC's ownership, elections, and circumstances. See our complete guide on [How LLCs Are Taxed] for a detailed explanation.

Default federal tax treatment for many LLCs generally passes income through to the owner or owners rather than imposing a separate federal income tax at the LLC level. State and local taxes, employment taxes, and other obligations may still apply.

Default Tax: Pass-Through and Self-Employment Tax

Many LLCs receive pass-through tax treatment, meaning business income generally flows through to the owner or owners rather than being taxed at the LLC level. Self-employment tax may also apply, depending on the LLC's tax classification and the owner's circumstances.

⚠️ The Self-Employment Tax Reality

Self-employment tax rules can be complex and do not apply identically to every LLC owner. An S-corp election may provide different tax treatment in some situations, but whether it makes financial sense depends on factors such as profit, reasonable compensation, payroll costs, and other tax considerations.

The S-Corp Election: When It May Affect Your Tax Bill

An LLC that meets the requirements can elect S corporation tax treatment by filing the appropriate election with the IRS. An S corporation generally pays its owner-employees through a reasonable salary, which is subject to employment taxes, while qualifying distributions may receive different tax treatment.

Whether an S-corp election reduces overall taxes depends on the owner's profit, reasonable compensation, payroll costs, administrative expenses, and other tax factors. There is no universal $60,000 or $80,000 profit threshold at which an S-corp election becomes beneficial.

How to Form an LLC — 5 Steps

The Formation Process From Start to Approval

Forming an LLC involves a few key steps, although requirements, fees, and processing times vary by state. Use these five steps as a general roadmap, then confirm the exact requirements with your state's official agency.

1
Choose Your State & Check Name Availability
Choose the state where you plan to form your LLC, considering where your business operates and any foreign-qualification requirements. Then check your state's business-name database to confirm your proposed name meets state naming and availability rules.
2
Appoint a Registered Agent
LLCs generally must designate a registered agent that meets the state's requirements. The agent receives official legal and state documents on the LLC's behalf. Depending on state rules, you may be able to serve as your own agent or hire a professional registered-agent service.
3
File the Formation Document & Pay the State Fee
Submit your state's required formation document, such as Articles of Organization or a Certificate of Formation, and pay the applicable filing fee. Costs, filing methods, required information, and processing times vary by state. Some states also offer expedited processing for an additional fee.
4
Create an Operating Agreement
An operating agreement can document ownership, management, voting, profit distributions, and other rules for running the LLC. Requirements vary by state, and the agreement generally does not need to be filed with the state. Professional review may be useful for complex or multi-member businesses.
5
Get an EIN if Applicable & Open a Business Bank Account
An EIN is available directly from the IRS at no charge when your LLC needs one. After formation, consider opening a dedicated business bank account and keeping business and personal finances separate. Maintaining clear records and respecting the LLC's separate legal status can help preserve the distinction between the business and its owners.
🛠️ Do It Yourself or Use a Service?

You can generally form an LLC yourself by following your state's filing instructions, without paying for a formation service. A service may be useful if you want filing assistance, a professional registered agent, privacy-focused address options, or additional administrative support. Do You Actually Need an LLC?

Consider Liability, Cost & Your Business Situation

Not every business needs an LLC immediately, and there is no universal revenue level that determines when you should form one. Consider factors such as your liability exposure, business activity, personal assets, ownership structure, state requirements, and tax situation.

Consider Forming an LLC If These Apply
🟢
You Have Personal Assets to Protect
An LLC may help separate eligible business liabilities from your personal assets, subject to important exceptions.
🟢
You Work With Clients or Customers
Contracts, products, services, and business activities can create potential liability exposure.
🟢
You Hire Employees or Contractors
Employment and workplace responsibilities can create additional business risks and compliance obligations.
🟢
You Want a Separate Business Structure
An LLC can provide a formal ownership, management, and financial structure for your business.
🟢
You Want Flexible Tax Options
Depending on eligibility, an LLC can use different federal tax classifications.

When You May Choose to Wait

If you're only testing an idea, have limited business activity, and face relatively low liability exposure, operating as a sole proprietor may be a reasonable starting point. However, review your state's rules, business risks, insurance needs, and tax considerations before deciding.

There is no one-size-fits-all revenue threshold for forming an LLC. As your business grows or your liability exposure changes, reassess whether your current structure still fits your situation.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Frequently Asked Questions

Understanding What an LLC Is

01 What does LLC stand for?

LLC stands for Limited Liability Company. It is a business entity created under state law that can provide limited liability protection for its owners while offering flexible management and several federal tax-classification options.

02 Is an LLC a corporation?

No. An LLC and a corporation are different types of business entities. Both can provide limited liability protection, but their ownership, management, filing, and tax rules differ. LLCs generally use members rather than shareholders and may have fewer formal governance requirements, depending on state law.

03 How is an LLC different from a sole proprietorship?

A sole proprietorship generally does not create a separate legal entity from its owner. An LLC is a separate legal entity under state law, which can help separate business obligations from the owner's personal assets. However, LLC liability protection is not absolute and exceptions can apply.

LLC Taxes and Costs

04 How is an LLC taxed?

Federal tax treatment depends on the LLC's ownership and elections. A single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity by default, while a multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership unless it elects corporate taxation. Eligible LLCs may also elect S-corporation treatment.

Self-employment and other taxes depend on the LLC's tax classification, ownership, income, and circumstances. There is no single tax rate that applies to every LLC owner.

05 How much does it cost to form an LLC?

State filing fees vary significantly by state. Additional costs may include required initial filings, publication requirements, recurring reports or taxes, registered-agent services, licenses, and optional formation services.

06 Can one person form an LLC?

Yes. A single-member LLC can have one owner. It can provide a formal business structure and limited liability protection while generally receiving simplified federal tax treatment by default. State requirements and tax rules still apply.

LLC Formation and Compliance

07 How long does it take to form an LLC?

Processing times vary by state, filing method, and workload. Some states offer online or expedited processing, while standard or paper filings may take longer.

Always check the current processing time and expedited options with the relevant state agency.

08 Do I need an operating agreement?

An operating agreement can be useful even when it is not required to be filed with the state. It can document ownership, management, voting, profit distributions, and other internal rules.

For a single-member LLC, it can also help document how the business is organized and operated. Requirements and legal effects vary by state.

09 What happens to an LLC if an owner dies?

The result depends on state law, the LLC's operating agreement, ownership structure, and estate-planning documents. An operating agreement may establish what happens to a member's ownership interest, including transfer, buy-sell, or succession provisions.

For a multi-member LLC, the agreement may also address how the remaining members handle a deceased member's interest. Review the applicable state rules and obtain professional advice for estate or succession planning.
