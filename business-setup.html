Business Setup After LLC — Complete Checklist 2026

Essential Post-Formation Steps to Organize Your Business & Stay Compliant

Completing your business setup after LLC formation is an important part of getting your company ready to operate. State approval creates the LLC, but it does not automatically complete every federal, state, banking, tax, licensing, or internal business requirement that may apply to you.

The steps you need depend on your LLC's state, ownership structure, activities, tax classification, and whether you have employees. Some steps can be completed at no additional cost, while others may involve state fees, licenses, professional services, or recurring business expenses.

This guide covers the main post-formation steps in a practical order. Each section links to a more detailed resource where applicable. Whether you just formed your LLC or are still comparing LLC formation services, use this checklist to organize the key tasks that may apply after state approval.

Step 1: Get Your EIN from the IRS

An Employer Identification Number (EIN) is a federal tax identification number issued by the IRS. It is commonly used by businesses for federal tax purposes, banking, payroll, and other business activities. The IRS does not charge a fee for an EIN application when you apply directly.

Many LLCs need an EIN, but it is not automatically required for every LLC. The requirement can depend on the LLC's tax classification, whether it has employees, and other federal tax circumstances. Banks may also have their own documentation requirements for opening a business account.

For most LLC owners who need an EIN, applying after the LLC has been legally formed can make the process more straightforward because you can provide the entity's approved legal name and formation information.

What You Need to Apply

EIN Application Requirements — 2026
✓ Responsible party information The IRS requires the name and taxpayer identification information of the responsible party who controls or manages the entity.
✓ LLC's legal name Use the entity's legal name as established in its formation documents and provide any required trade-name information.
✓ Business address Provide the address requested by the IRS for the business and responsible party information. A P.O. Box may not satisfy every address field or requirement.
✓ Entity information You will provide information about the LLC, including its state of formation and the number of members where requested.
✓ Tax classification information The application asks questions that help determine the LLC's federal tax treatment and the reason for applying.
⚠️ Never pay a third party simply to obtain an EIN when you can apply directly. The IRS provides EINs at no charge through its official application process. Some formation companies offer EIN application assistance as a paid add-on, but that fee is for the provider's service rather than the EIN itself.

For the current application process and availability, use the official IRS EIN application rather than relying on a third-party website.

EIN and Your LLC Tax Status

Receiving an EIN does not by itself determine how your LLC is taxed. Federal tax classification depends on the LLC's ownership and elections made with the IRS.

Generally, a domestic single-member LLC is treated as a disregarded entity for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. A domestic multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership unless it elects to be taxed as a corporation.

An LLC may also be eligible to elect S-corporation tax treatment if it meets the applicable IRS requirements. There is no universal income level at which an S-corp election automatically becomes beneficial. The potential tax effect depends on factors such as profit, reasonable compensation, payroll taxes, owner circumstances, administrative costs, and applicable federal and state rules.

Step 2: Create Your Operating Agreement

An operating agreement is an internal document that establishes how an LLC will be owned, managed, and operated. State requirements vary, and many states do not require an LLC to file an operating agreement with the state.

Even when a written agreement is not mandatory, it can provide a clear record of the company's internal rules and help distinguish the LLC's business arrangements from the owners' personal affairs. This can be particularly useful for multi-member LLCs, where ownership, voting, distributions, and member responsibilities should be documented clearly.

What Your Operating Agreement Should Cover

Operating Agreement — Key Sections
✓ Ownership percentages Identifies each member's ownership interest and can help establish how ownership changes are handled.
✓ Management structure Explains whether the LLC is member-managed or manager-managed and identifies relevant management authority.
✓ Profit and loss distribution Establishes how profits and losses are allocated and how distributions may be made.
✓ Voting rights and decisions Sets out voting procedures and identifies decisions that require member approval under the agreement.
✓ Member exit procedures Addresses transfers, withdrawal, death, disability, or other events affecting a member's ownership interest.
💡 Single-member LLCs can benefit from an operating agreement too. Even when there is only one owner, a written agreement can document the LLC's structure, management, and ownership. It can also provide useful documentation for banks, counterparties, and other third parties.

An operating agreement is not a guarantee of liability protection and does not by itself prevent a court from disregarding an LLC. Maintaining appropriate business records, keeping business and personal finances separate, following applicable laws, and properly operating the LLC are also important considerations.

Step 3: Open a Business Bank Account

A dedicated business bank account is an important part of maintaining clear separation between an LLC and its owners. Keeping business income and expenses in a separate account makes bookkeeping easier and creates clearer financial records for the company.

Mixing personal and business finances does not automatically cause an LLC's liability protection to disappear, but poor separation of finances can create legal and accounting complications. Following the LLC's organizational requirements, maintaining appropriate records, and treating the business as a separate entity are important parts of responsible LLC administration.

Opening an account is often straightforward, although each bank has its own eligibility and documentation requirements. Some online business banking providers offer accounts with no monthly maintenance fee, while traditional banks may charge monthly fees or offer fee waivers based on account activity or balances.

What You May Need to Open the Account

Business Bank Account Requirements
✓ EIN confirmation or IRS EIN information Many business banks request an EIN, although requirements can vary by bank and business structure. Some single-member LLCs may have different federal tax identification circumstances.
✓ Approved Articles of Organization State formation documents or approval information may be requested to verify that the LLC exists.
✓ Operating Agreement Some banks request an operating agreement, while others may require different ownership or authorization documents.
✓ Valid government-issued ID The bank generally verifies the identity of the owners, responsible party, managers, or other authorized individuals as required by its procedures.
✓ Business address Provide an address that meets the bank's requirements. A registered-agent address or virtual business address may not be accepted for every banking purpose, so confirm the bank's address rules before applying.

Step 4: Get Your Business License

Forming an LLC and obtaining a business license or permit are separate processes. Creating the LLC establishes the business entity under state law; licenses and permits address whether the business is authorized to conduct particular activities in a specific jurisdiction.

There is no single nationwide business license that applies to every LLC. Depending on the business, you may need federal, state, county, city, professional, industry-specific, sales-tax, or home-occupation approvals.

License Requirements by Level

Business License Types — Which Ones May Apply
- Federal license or permit Certain regulated activities may require federal registration, licensing, or permits. Requirements depend on the industry and activity.
- State professional license Licensed professions such as law, medicine, accounting, contracting, and real estate may have separate professional licensing requirements.
- General business license Some cities, counties, and states require a general business license; others do not. Check the jurisdiction where your business operates.
- Sales tax registration Businesses selling taxable goods or services may need to register with the applicable state or local tax authority. Requirements vary by state and by what you sell.
- Home occupation permit A city or county may require a home-occupation permit or impose zoning restrictions when a business operates from a residence. Requirements vary by location and business activity.

Find the Licenses That Apply to Your Business

Not sure which licenses or permits apply? Start with the government agencies responsible for your business location and industry. Our Secretary of State Business Search can help you locate state business information, while our business license guide explains the main categories to investigate.

Licensed professionals — including doctors, lawyers, dentists, and certain other regulated professionals — should also check whether their state permits the profession to operate through a standard LLC or requires a Professional LLC (PLLC) or another professional entity.

💡 Freelancers and online businesses are not automatically exempt from licensing requirements. Some may have relatively few licensing obligations, while others may need local business licenses, professional credentials, sales-tax registration, zoning approval, or industry-specific permits. Check the requirements for your actual location, business activity, and customers.

Step 5: Get Business Insurance

Your LLC can help separate your personal assets from many business liabilities, but the LLC structure does not replace business insurance. Insurance can provide coverage for specific risks such as bodily injury, property damage, professional errors, or employee-related claims, depending on the policy and its exclusions.

The coverage your business needs depends on what you do, where you operate, whether you have employees, and whether a client, landlord, lender, contract, or government agency requires a particular policy. Some businesses may need only limited coverage, while others may need several policies.

General Liability: Can help cover certain third-party claims involving bodily injury, property damage, or related incidents, subject to policy terms and exclusions.

Professional Liability (E&O): Can help cover certain claims alleging professional mistakes, negligence, or failure to deliver professional services as expected. It is particularly relevant to consultants, designers, accountants, agencies, technology businesses, and other professional-service businesses.

Workers' Compensation: Rules vary significantly by state and can depend on employee count, business structure, industry, and ownership status. Do not assume that having an LLC automatically means workers' compensation is required — or that having no employees automatically eliminates every requirement.

💡 A practical starting point: Review the risks associated with your actual business activity rather than buying a fixed package simply because you formed an LLC. A consultant may have different insurance needs from an online retailer, contractor, landlord, or professional practice. Also check whether contracts, leases, clients, licensing agencies, or state law require specific coverage.

Insurance pricing varies by industry, location, revenue, payroll, coverage limits, deductibles, claims history, and other factors. Instead of assuming a universal monthly cost, compare quotes and policy limits for the coverage your business actually needs.

State-Specific Insurance Requirements

Insurance requirements can vary substantially by state and business type. Workers' compensation is one of the most common examples: state rules can depend on factors such as the number of employees, industry, ownership structure, and whether certain workers are exempt.

New Jersey, for example, has specific workers' compensation requirements and penalties for noncompliance. Because these rules can change and exceptions may apply, verify the current requirements through the relevant New Jersey government agency before relying on a specific penalty amount.

Step 6: Get a Virtual Business Address

Your LLC may require several different types of addresses, and they do not all serve the same purpose. Depending on your state and filing, an address can appear in public business records, while other addresses may be used for registered-agent service, business correspondence, banking, licensing, or tax purposes.

Using your home address may therefore create some public-record or privacy considerations, but the exact exposure depends on your state's filing requirements and which address you provide. A virtual business address can be one option for separating your residential address from certain business correspondence or public-facing uses, but it does not automatically keep your home address out of every government record.

A virtual address can provide a commercial mailing location without requiring you to rent a traditional office. However, whether you can use it for a particular purpose depends on the state, agency, bank, service provider, and type of address.

⚠️ Before using your home address, check the specific filing and address rules for your state. Some business records are public, but not every address used by an LLC is necessarily displayed in the same way. If privacy is important, consider whether a registered agent, commercial address, virtual mailbox, or another address solution is appropriate for each specific use.

Changing an address later may require amendments or updates with the state, IRS, banks, licensing agencies, vendors, and other services. Earlier records may remain available in government archives or third-party databases, depending on the jurisdiction and provider. A later change therefore may not erase historical information that was previously public.

Stay Compliant After Your Business Setup

Your business setup after LLC formation does not end with the six steps above. Ongoing compliance requirements can include state reports, franchise taxes, registered-agent maintenance, licenses, tax filings, and other obligations depending on your state and business activities. Missing a required deadline can result in fees, penalties, loss of good standing, or other consequences under state law.

Two areas are especially important to monitor after formation: state compliance filings and registered-agent information. However, the exact requirements and deadlines vary by state, so your LLC may have additional obligations.

Annual Report

Many states require LLCs to file an annual or biennial report — sometimes called a Statement of Information, Periodic Report, or Annual Statement — and some states charge a filing fee or separate franchise tax. Other states use different reporting schedules or do not require a traditional annual report.

Deadlines and costs vary significantly depending on where you formed your LLC. For example, California generally imposes an $800 annual franchise tax on many LLCs, while New Mexico does not impose a comparable annual LLC franchise tax. However, businesses in New Mexico can still have other state, local, tax, or licensing obligations depending on their circumstances.

To track your specific deadline and avoid missing it, use our free LLC Annual Report Tracker. Additionally, our full annual report guide covers deadlines, fees, and filing considerations for all 50 states.

Registered Agent

An LLC generally must maintain a registered agent that meets the requirements of the state where the LLC is registered. The registered agent receives legal documents and certain official state notices on behalf of the business. In many states, the registered agent must maintain a physical address in that state and be available to receive service of process during required hours.

Keeping your registered-agent information current is important. If legal papers or official notices cannot be properly delivered because the information is outdated, your business may miss important response deadlines and could face procedural consequences, depending on the type of notice and applicable state or court rules.

A professional registered-agent service is one option for maintaining the required address and receiving official documents, but it is not the only option in every state. An LLC owner may be able to serve as their own registered agent if they meet the state's requirements.

See our registered agent guide for more information about registered-agent requirements, address considerations, privacy, and costs. You can also review our registered agent service comparison to compare publicly advertised pricing and service features.

💡 Registered agent and business address are not necessarily the same thing: A registered agent's address is used for service of process and certain official notices. A virtual business address or mailbox may serve a different purpose, and whether a particular address can be used on a state filing depends on the applicable state rules.

If you choose a commercial registered-agent service, compare the first-year price, renewal cost, address/privacy features, mail handling, compliance reminders, cancellation terms, and exactly what is included. Provider offers can change, so verify the current terms before purchasing.

Frequently Asked Questions

1 What is the first step in business setup after forming an LLC?
For many LLCs, obtaining an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS is an important early step. The IRS provides EINs directly at no charge, and eligible applicants can receive an EIN online after completing the application.

Whether your LLC needs an EIN depends on factors such as its tax classification, employees, and other circumstances. Many LLCs obtain one because banks, tax filings, payroll systems, or other business requirements may call for it.

For a straightforward domestic LLC, applying after the state has approved the formation can make the process easier to organize. If you are a non-U.S. applicant or your principal place of business is outside the United States, IRS application procedures can differ. See our EIN guide for the applicable process.
2 Do I need a business bank account for my LLC?
A separate business bank account is generally a practical way to keep business and personal transactions organized, but there is no single bank-account requirement that applies identically to every LLC.

Keeping finances separate can make bookkeeping, tax reporting, and documentation easier and can help demonstrate that the business is being operated separately from its owners. Mixing personal and business funds does not automatically eliminate an LLC's liability protection, but poor recordkeeping and failure to maintain appropriate separation can create legal and accounting complications.

Bank requirements also vary. A bank may request your EIN, Articles of Organization, Operating Agreement, identification, business address, or other documentation.

See our business bank account guide for account requirements and features to compare.
3 How much does the business setup after an LLC cost?
There is no single cost that applies to every LLC. Some post-formation items can be free, while others depend on your state, industry, business structure, employees, insurance needs, and service providers.

For example:
  • EIN: Free when obtained directly from the IRS.
  • Business bank account: Fees and minimum-balance requirements vary by bank and account.
  • Business licenses and permits: Costs vary by state, city, county, industry, and type of license.
  • Insurance: Premiums vary based on business activity, location, coverage limits, claims history, payroll, and other factors.
  • Registered agent: May be free if you qualify to serve yourself, or may involve an annual service fee.
  • Annual reports and state taxes: Depend on the state and your LLC's circumstances.
  • Operating agreement: Can often be prepared using a template, although more complex businesses may choose professional assistance.
Use our free Startup Cost Estimator to organize the potential costs for your particular setup rather than relying on a single national estimate.
4 Do I need a business license after forming my LLC?
Possibly. Forming an LLC creates a legal business entity, while a license or permit may authorize a particular business activity. They are separate requirements.

There is no universal nationwide business license requirement for every LLC. Depending on the business, you may need a state professional license, local business license, sales-tax registration, industry permit, health permit, zoning approval, or another authorization.

Your actual requirements depend on your location and activities. An online business or freelancer is not automatically exempt, and a business operating from home may still have local licensing or zoning requirements.

See our full business license guide for more information.
5 Do I need an operating agreement for my LLC?
Many states do not require an operating agreement to be filed with the state, but an operating agreement can be useful for documenting how the LLC will be owned and managed.

It can establish rules covering ownership percentages, member contributions, management, voting, profit and loss allocations, member changes, dispute procedures, and dissolution.

A single-member LLC can also benefit from having an operating agreement because it documents the business's structure and internal rules. For multi-member LLCs, the document can be particularly useful for establishing the members' agreed arrangements.

Without an operating agreement, applicable state default rules may govern areas that the members have not otherwise addressed. The specific consequences vary by state and LLC structure.
6 Should I get a virtual address for my LLC?
A virtual business address may be useful if you work from home and want to separate your residential address from certain business correspondence or public-facing uses. However, it is not required for every LLC and does not automatically keep your home address out of every government record.

Address rules vary by state and by the purpose for which the address is being used. A registered-agent address, principal business address, mailing address, and virtual mailbox may serve different functions, and a bank, government agency, or licensing authority may have its own requirements.

If privacy is one of your priorities, review the specific address requirements before filing rather than assuming that a virtual address can be substituted everywhere.

Not sure whether you need one? Read our guide: Do You Need a Virtual Address for Your LLC?
7 What is the difference between a virtual address and a registered agent?
They serve different purposes.

A registered agent is designated to receive service of process and certain official state notices for an LLC. State rules determine who can serve as the registered agent and what address requirements apply.

A virtual business address or mailbox is generally used for business correspondence, mail handling, or certain public-facing business purposes. Whether a particular virtual address can be used on a state filing, bank application, license, or tax document depends on the applicable requirements.

Some commercial providers offer both services, but they should not automatically be treated as interchangeable.

See our full comparison: Virtual Address vs. Registered Agent.
8 How do I pay myself from my LLC?
How you take money from an LLC depends largely on how the LLC is taxed.

A single-member LLC that is taxed by default as a disregarded entity generally does not treat an owner's draw as a wage for federal income-tax purposes. The owner may transfer money from the business to themselves, while the business's income and expenses are generally reported according to the applicable tax rules.

An LLC taxed as an S corporation has different payroll requirements. Shareholder-employees generally need to receive reasonable compensation through payroll before taking certain distributions. The appropriate compensation depends on the facts of the business and should not be based on a universal percentage or salary figure.

Tax elections can have significant consequences, so consider discussing your situation with a qualified tax professional.

Our LLC vs. S-Corp guide explains the structural and tax differences, while our S-Corp Tax Calculator can help you explore potential scenarios. Calculator results are estimates and are not tax advice.
9 What is the BOI report and do I need to file one in 2026?
The federal Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) rules changed substantially in 2025 and again in 2026.

As of August 14, 2026, FinCEN's final rule exempts companies created in the United States from the federal BOI reporting requirement. U.S. companies therefore do not need to file BOI reports under the current rule.

Certain foreign companies that are formed under foreign law and registered to do business in the United States can still fall within the reporting requirements if they do not qualify for another exemption. The current rules also provide that reporting companies do not report BOI for U.S. persons.

Because FinCEN's BOI rules have changed multiple times, do not rely on older LLC guides that still describe a general BOI filing requirement for domestic U.S. LLCs. Check the current FinCEN BOI guidance before filing or paying anyone for BOI assistance.
