Single-Member LLC: How It Works in 2026

A single-member LLC (SMLLC) is a limited liability company formed under state law with exactly one owner, called a member. It is a common structure for freelancers, consultants, independent contractors, online businesses, investors, and other businesses operated by one owner.


A single-member LLC can provide a liability barrier between the business and its owner, subject to state law and important exceptions. At the federal level, the IRS generally treats a domestic single-member LLC as a disregarded entity by default for income-tax purposes unless the LLC makes an election to be taxed as a corporation. That distinction is important: an LLC can be legally separate from its owner under state law while being disregarded as a separate entity for federal income-tax purposes.

What Is a Single-Member LLC

A single-member LLC, often abbreviated SMLLC, is a limited liability company formed under state law with exactly one owner. The LLC can generally enter contracts, own property, maintain its own bank account, conduct business, and incur obligations in its own name. For federal income-tax purposes, a domestic single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity unless it elects to be classified as a corporation.

This is why an SMLLC can be both a separate legal entity under state law and disregarded as a separate entity for federal income-tax purposes. "Disregarded entity" refers to federal income-tax classification only. It does not mean the LLC disappears legally. For a broader introduction, see our guide on what an LLC is.

Default Tax: Schedule C and Self-Employment Tax

Under the default federal classification, a single-member LLC does not pay federal income tax at the entity level. Instead, the owner's business income and expenses are generally reported on the owner's individual return, commonly Schedule C, with self-employment tax generally calculated on Schedule SE. The self-employment tax rate is generally 15.3%, but the calculation uses 92.35% of net profit. For 2026, the Social Security wage base is $184,500.

Liability Protection: What It Covers (and What It Doesn't)

A properly maintained LLC can create a legal separation between the business and its owner. Generally, business debts and liabilities belong to the LLC rather than automatically becoming the owner's personal obligations. However, limited liability is not an absolute guarantee that personal assets can never be reached.

When the Liability Shield Can Be Challenged

Courts can consider factors such as whether the LLC was properly formed and maintained, whether the owner and business were treated as separate, whether the owner misused the entity, whether required state filings were maintained, and whether the owner personally guaranteed an obligation. The legal standard varies by jurisdiction.

Practical Priority: Keep business and personal finances separate. A dedicated business bank account is one of the simplest ways to maintain clear financial records. It helps document the distinction between the owner and the company and can reduce avoidable confusion.

How to Form a Single-Member LLC — Step by Step

1
Choose a unique business name

Your LLC name must satisfy your state's naming rules and generally must be distinguishable from existing registered entities. Check your state's official business-entity database before filing.

2
Appoint a registered agent

Every domestic LLC generally must designate a registered agent. A professional registered-agent service can be useful for privacy and document monitoring. Compare options in our LLC formation services guide.

3
File Articles of Organization

Submit your state's required formation document. State filing fees vary substantially. See our how to start an LLC guide or the LLC formation services guide if you prefer a commercial provider.

4
Create an operating agreement

An operating agreement sets out how the LLC is owned and operated. Even where not mandatory, it can document the owner's intent, management structure, and procedures for important business decisions.

5
Get your EIN from the IRS

An EIN is issued by the IRS at no charge. See our EIN guide. Use the IRS's official EIN application process rather than paying a third party.

6
Open a dedicated business bank account

Open a dedicated business bank account and keep business transactions separate from personal spending.

S Corp Election for Single-Member LLCs

An eligible single-member LLC can elect to be taxed as an S corporation using Form 2553. The LLC remains an LLC under state law; only the federal tax treatment changes. The owner must receive reasonable compensation as wages for services provided to the business. There is no universal profit threshold at which an S-corp election automatically becomes worthwhile.

The 2026 S-corp election deadline for a calendar-year taxpayer was March 16, 2026 (because March 15 fell on Sunday). See our LLC vs S Corp guide and S-Corp Tax Calculator for additional information.

Frequently Asked Questions — Single-Member LLC

Does a single-member LLC need to file a separate federal income-tax return?

Usually not under the default federal classification. A domestic single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity, so an individual owner commonly reports the business activity on Schedule C with Form 1040. However, other federal filings may apply depending on the business. If the LLC elects S-corporation or C-corporation taxation, separate entity-level federal returns generally become applicable.

What is the self-employment tax rate for a single-member LLC in 2026?

The general self-employment tax rate is 15.3%, consisting of Social Security and Medicare taxes. The calculation generally applies to 92.35% of net earnings from self-employment, subject to the applicable Social Security wage base and other rules. For 2026, the Social Security wage base is $184,500. Additional Medicare tax can apply to certain higher-income taxpayers.

Is a single-member LLC the same as a sole proprietorship for taxes?

Not exactly, although a default-taxed single-member LLC can have similar federal income-tax reporting. Both can commonly use Schedule C when owned by an individual, but the LLC is a state-law entity that can provide limited liability and can generally elect corporate tax treatment if eligible.

Do I need an EIN for a single-member LLC with no employees?

Not always. A single-member LLC without employees may, in certain circumstances, use the owner's Social Security number for federal income-tax purposes. However, obtaining an EIN is often useful for banking, vendors, state registrations, hiring employees, and future tax elections. An EIN is free from the IRS.

Can a single-member LLC elect S-corp taxation?

Yes, if it meets the applicable eligibility requirements. The LLC generally files Form 2553 to elect S-corporation status. The LLC remains an LLC under state law; the election changes its federal tax treatment.

Can I convert a sole proprietorship to a single-member LLC?

Generally, yes. A common approach is to form the LLC with the state, obtain any necessary EIN and registrations, open business banking, update contracts and licenses, and move the business activity into the LLC. Some states provide specific statutory conversion procedures, while others may require forming a new LLC and transferring the business.

Can a single-member LLC have employees?

Yes. An LLC can hire employees regardless of whether it has one or multiple members. Once you become an employer, federal and state employment-tax, payroll, wage, workers' compensation, and reporting requirements may apply. An EIN is generally required for an employer.

How do I pay myself from a single-member LLC?

Under the default tax classification, an individual owner commonly takes money through an owner's draw rather than treating the payment as a W-2 salary. The owner's federal tax liability is generally based on the business's taxable profit rather than simply the amount withdrawn. If the LLC elects S-corporation taxation, the owner generally needs to follow S-corp payroll and reasonable-compensation rules.

Can a married couple form a single-member LLC?

Possibly for federal tax purposes in certain circumstances in community-property states. The IRS has special rules for spouses who jointly own an LLC in community-property states: Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington, and Wisconsin. Eligibility depends on the ownership arrangement, state law, and applicable IRS requirements.

What happens if I miss my state's annual report deadline?

The consequences depend on the state. A missed filing can result in late fees, loss of good standing, administrative dissolution, or other penalties. Some states provide reinstatement procedures. Administrative dissolution can affect the LLC's legal status and should be addressed promptly.
