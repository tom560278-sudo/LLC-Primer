How to Pay Yourself From an LLC (2026)

How you pay yourself from an LLC depends almost entirely on how the LLC is classified for federal tax purposes. A single-member LLC with default classification generally uses owner draws. A multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership distributes money according to the operating agreement. An LLC that has elected S corporation treatment generally must pay a reasonable W-2 salary before any additional distributions are taken.

This guide explains each method, the tax treatment of each approach, how to handle estimated taxes when there is no withholding, and what to watch out for when an S-corp election is involved.

How the LLC's Tax Classification Determines How You Pay Yourself

Method 1: Owner Draws (Disregarded Entity LLCs)

A domestic single-member LLC with default federal classification is generally treated as a disregarded entity. The owner is not an employee of the LLC for federal income-tax purposes. Instead, the owner takes money out of the business account as an owner draw.

How Owner Draws Work

  • Transfer funds from the LLC business account to your personal account
  • Record each draw in your bookkeeping records as an owner's draw/distribution
  • Do not withhold taxes from draws — you are responsible for making estimated tax payments
  • The amount you draw does not directly affect your net self-employment income for tax purposes
  • Self-employment tax is based on the LLC's net business income, not the amount you drew
The tax is on profit, not draws: If the LLC earns $80,000 in net profit but you only draw $30,000, self-employment tax is still generally calculated on $80,000 of net earnings from self-employment, not $30,000.

Estimated Tax Payments

Draws are generally not subject to automatic withholding. Federal estimated income tax and self-employment tax may need to be paid quarterly. The standard 2026 due dates are April 15, June 16, September 15, and January 15, 2027. State estimated-tax due dates can differ. Underpayment penalties may apply when estimated payments are not made correctly.

Method 2: Partnership Distributions (Multi-Member LLCs)

A domestic multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects otherwise. The LLC files Form 1065 and provides each member with a Schedule K-1. Members receive their share of income, deductions, gains, and losses based on their ownership percentage or other agreed arrangement documented in the operating agreement.

How Partnership Distributions Work

  • The operating agreement generally establishes when and how distributions can be made
  • Distributions must be tracked against each member's capital account basis
  • A distribution in excess of basis can have tax consequences
  • Guaranteed payments to partners are a separate category and are generally subject to self-employment tax
  • Each member pays estimated taxes on their allocable share of partnership income

For a detailed explanation of self-employment tax in multi-member LLCs, see our LLC taxation guide.

Method 3: W-2 Salary + Distributions (S-Corp Election)

An eligible LLC can elect S corporation treatment. When an LLC taxed as an S corporation has an owner who performs services for the company, that owner generally must receive reasonable compensation as W-2 wages before receiving additional non-wage distributions.

Reasonable Compensation Is a Legal Requirement, Not a Tax Choice. IRS guidance consistently requires that S corporation shareholder-employees receive reasonable compensation for services before non-wage distributions are taken. The reasonable salary should reflect what a similarly qualified third-party employee would receive for similar work.

How S-Corp Owner Payment Works

  • Set up payroll for the business
  • Pay yourself a reasonable salary through payroll with appropriate withholding
  • File Form 941 quarterly
  • Issue Form W-2 by January 31 each year
  • Any additional distributions can be taken according to the S-corporation rules
  • Document compensation decisions and record them appropriately

How Much Should You Pay Yourself?

Before Paying Yourself, Estimate Your Tax Liability

Many self-employed LLC owners set aside a portion of each payment received before making any draws. A common planning approach is to set aside 25% to 35% of net business income, depending on the owner's tax bracket, state tax, and available deductions. This is a rough estimate, not a rule. A tax professional can help determine a more accurate quarterly estimate based on your actual situation.

Maintain Enough for Business Operations

Pay yourself after confirming that the LLC has enough cash to cover upcoming operating expenses, vendor payments, payroll (if any), quarterly estimated taxes, required state fees, and other obligations. Depleting the business account with excessive draws and then covering business costs personally can undermine financial separation.

Recordkeeping for Owner Draws and Distributions

  • Record each draw or distribution in your bookkeeping system with the date, amount, and category (owner draw or distributions)
  • Use a dedicated business bank account to document the transfer clearly
  • Maintain year-end capital-account records for partnership LLCs
  • For S corporations, maintain payroll records, W-2s, and quarterly 941 filings
  • Keep all distributions backed by an operating agreement or resolution

Frequently Asked Questions

01 How do I pay myself from an LLC?

The method depends on the LLC's federal tax classification. A single-member disregarded LLC generally pays through owner draws taken from the business account. A multi-member partnership LLC distributes according to the operating agreement. An LLC taxed as an S corporation generally pays a reasonable W-2 salary, plus potential additional distributions.

02 Is there a required amount for owner draws from an LLC?

There is no minimum or maximum draw required by federal law for a disregarded entity or partnership LLC. However, federal and state estimated taxes should be considered, because draws are not automatically subject to withholding.

03 Does taking an owner draw trigger self-employment tax?

Self-employment tax is generally applied to net earnings from self-employment reported on Schedule SE. Simply making a draw does not automatically trigger SE tax; the tax is based on the net business income and the entity's tax classification.

04 What is a reasonable salary for an LLC taxed as an S corporation?

Reasonable compensation considers the services performed, experience, time committed, market rates for similar work, and what the business would otherwise pay for those services. It should not be designed solely to minimize wages. The IRS actively examines situations where S-corp shareholder-employees receive unreasonably low wages.

05 Can I take a draw from an S-corp LLC?

An owner of an S corporation who performs services for the company must generally be paid a reasonable W-2 wage. After paying required compensation, the owner may also be able to receive additional distributions. Those distributions are generally not subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes, but distributions cannot replace required reasonable compensation.

06 Do I owe self-employment tax on distributions from a multi-member LLC?

The self-employment treatment for LLC partnership income is a more complex area. For most general-partner-equivalent members who are actively involved in the business, the net earnings may be subject to self-employment tax. The rules are different for limited partners and for members classified differently under applicable regulations.

07 Do I need a payroll provider to pay myself a salary from an LLC?

If your LLC is taxed as an S corporation or C corporation and you are required to pay yourself wages, you generally need to handle payroll functions including withholding, payroll taxes, quarterly deposits, and Forms 941 and W-2. Payroll software or a professional payroll service can help manage these requirements.

08 Does my owner draw affect the LLC's bank account balance?

Yes. Any money you take out of the business account reduces the available balance. Maintaining enough cash to pay business obligations, taxes, and operating expenses before taking a draw is an important part of managing the business.

09 How much should I save for taxes?

There is no universal percentage, but many self-employed individuals set aside at least 25% to 35% of net business income to cover federal income tax and self-employment tax, depending on the taxpayer's tax bracket, state, and other deductions. This is a planning heuristic, not a rule.
