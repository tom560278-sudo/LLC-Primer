How Are LLCs Taxed? (2026 Federal & State Tax Guide)

Pass-Through Tax Treatment, S-Corp & C-Corp Elections, QBI & Self-Employment Tax Breakdown

An LLC is a legal structure created under state law, but "LLC" is not a federal income-tax classification. For federal tax purposes, the IRS generally treats an LLC as a disregarded entity, a partnership, a C corporation, or—if it meets the eligibility rules and makes the election—an S corporation. That distinction matters because the filing forms, payroll rules, self-employment tax exposure, and deductions can change even though the business remains an LLC under state law.

For most owners, the first question is not "what tax rate does an LLC pay?" but which federal classification applies to this LLC right now? A one-owner LLC normally starts as a disregarded entity. An LLC with two or more members normally starts as a partnership. Corporate tax treatment requires an election, and S-corporation status adds a separate set of eligibility and compensation rules. This guide focuses on the 2026 tax year and uses current IRS and SSA figures verified in August 2026.

Last reviewed: August 23, 2026 · Source: IRS Revenue Procedures & Social Security Administration
⚠️ 2026 tax year does not mean “file the return in 2026”

Most calendar-year returns reporting 2026 income are filed in 2027. Estimated tax payments for 2026 are made during 2026 and January 2027, while the federal income-tax returns themselves are generally due in March or April 2027. Mixing those two timelines is an easy way to publish the wrong deadline.

Start Here: Which Federal Tax Treatment Applies?

The IRS classification rules start with the number of owners. A domestic LLC with one owner is generally disregarded for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. A domestic LLC with at least two members is generally treated as a partnership unless it elects corporate treatment. An eligible LLC can then elect S-corporation status by filing Form 2553. For a deeper explanation of the legal structure itself, use the LLC Guide hub.

💡 LEGAL ENTITY AND TAX CLASSIFICATION ARE DIFFERENT LAYERS

An LLC that elects corporate taxation does not automatically become a corporation under state law. The federal election changes how the IRS taxes the entity; it does not rewrite the company's state-law formation documents.

Single-Member LLC: What the Default Really Means

A single-member LLC owned by an individual is generally disregarded for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. The LLC's income and deductions are therefore reported on the owner's federal return—commonly Schedule C for an operating trade or business, although Schedule E or F can apply in other situations.

"Disregarded" does not mean tax-free. If the owner operates a trade or business, net earnings from self-employment are generally subject to self-employment tax. The headline rate is 15.3%, but it is not simply 15.3% of every dollar of Schedule C profit: the SE-tax computation generally starts with 92.35% of net earnings, the 12.4% Social Security component is capped at the annual wage base, and Medicare rules continue above that cap.

Assume an unmarried owner has $80,000 of Schedule C net profit, no other income, no net capital gain, and no other adjustments that change the example. The purpose here is to show the interaction among SE tax, the deductible half of SE tax, the 2026 standard deduction, and the QBI limitation—not to calculate the owner's final federal income-tax bill.

Multi-Member LLC: Pass-Through Does Not Mean “No Return”

A domestic multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership unless it elects corporate treatment. The partnership normally files Form 1065 and furnishes Schedule K-1 to its members. The partnership generally does not pay federal income tax itself; instead, tax items pass through and are reported by the members.

General rules for partners differ from the limited partner exception, and LLC member status under IRC §1402(a)(13) can be fact-specific.

One correction that matters for dormant entities: a domestic partnership is not required to file Form 1065 merely because it exists. The IRS instructions provide an exception when the partnership neither receives income nor incurs expenditures treated as deductions or credits for federal income-tax purposes. Special situations can still create a filing requirement.

Profit allocations can also be more nuanced than ownership percentages, but allocations must satisfy federal partnership-tax rules; they are not simply a free choice written into an operating agreement. If you are deciding how members receive money from the LLC, our separate guide on paying yourself from an LLC covers draws, guaranteed payments and S-corp wages without collapsing them into one rule.

S-Corp Election: What Changes—and What Does Not

An LLC eligible for S-corporation treatment can file Form 2553. The election generally preserves pass-through federal income-tax treatment while adding corporate payroll rules. A shareholder who performs services for the S corporation can be an employee, and the IRS requires reasonable compensation before non-wage distributions are used as a substitute for pay for those services.

That creates potential payroll-tax savings in some profitable owner-operated businesses, but there is no universal profit level at which an S-corp election automatically "wins." The result depends on reasonable salary, payroll and tax-preparation costs, state taxes, other wages, retirement and health-benefit choices, QBI limitations, and the stability of the business's profit.

QUESTION BUSINESS OWNERS REPEATEDLY ASK
“At what income does an S-corp actually start making sense?”
✓
There is no IRS break-even number. A $50,000 or $75,000 rule of thumb can be misleading because two owners with the same profit can have different reasonable salaries, state taxes and compliance costs. Compare the projected payroll taxes, reasonable compensation, QBI effect and annual administration cost using your own facts.

Eligibility is not automatic

An LLC cannot choose S-corp status merely because the owner wants payroll-tax savings. The entity must meet the S-corporation eligibility rules, including being domestic, having no more than 100 shareholders, having only eligible shareholders, having no nonresident-alien shareholders, and having only one class of stock for federal tax purposes. Form 2553 is the election form.

A SALARY PERCENTAGE IS NOT AN IRS SAFE HARBOR

The tax code does not provide a fixed "60/40" split or another universal reasonable compensation percentage. The IRS and courts look at facts and circumstances such as duties, experience, time devoted to the business, comparable compensation and how payments are structured.

C-Corp Treatment: A Different Taxpayer Enters the Picture

An LLC can elect to be treated as a corporation for federal tax purposes, commonly using Form 8832. If it is taxed as a C corporation, the entity files Form 1120 and is subject to the federal corporate income-tax regime. The current federal corporate rate is 21%.

The key trade-off is that C-corp income does not simply "pass through" in the same way as default LLC or S-corp income. If after-tax earnings are later distributed as dividends, shareholders may face a second layer of tax. Qualified dividends can be taxed at preferential federal rates when the requirements are met, but the exact shareholder result depends on income and holding-period rules.

There is also a legal distinction worth preserving: electing C-corp tax treatment does not convert the LLC into a state-law corporation. If a business is raising institutional capital or needs a conventional corporate equity structure, the legal-entity decision should be analyzed separately from the federal tax election rather than assuming Form 8832 accomplishes both.

The 2026 QBI Deduction: Permanent, but Still Conditional

The Section 199A qualified business income deduction is now permanent. Eligible owners of sole proprietorships, partnerships and S corporations can generally deduct up to 20% of qualified business income, subject to taxable-income limits and, at higher income levels, wage/property and specified-service-business rules. C-corporation income is not QBI.

For tax years beginning after 2025, the law also adds a minimum QBI deduction of $400 for qualifying taxpayers with at least $1,000 of QBI, subject to the statutory conditions. The familiar 20% figure is still a maximum framework, not a promise that every owner receives 20% of business profit.

Does QBI make the S-corp election pointless?

No. QBI and S-corporation payroll rules solve different tax questions. An S-corp shareholder's W-2 wages are not QBI, while eligible pass-through business income may qualify for the Section 199A deduction. Changing compensation can therefore affect both payroll taxes and the QBI computation. This is one reason a one-line "S-corp saves X%" formula is unreliable. The broader LLC tax benefits guide is the better place to compare these interactions in detail.

Self-Employment Tax and Deductions: Keep the Buckets Separate

For 2026, the Social Security portion of self-employment tax is subject to a $184,500 taxable maximum. Medicare has no comparable earnings cap, and the additional 0.9% Medicare tax can apply above the statutory income thresholds. A person who also earns W-2 wages needs to consider those wages when determining how much of the Social Security base remains available for self-employment income.

Tax deductions are often described as though they all reduce the same thing. They do not. It is more useful to separate expenses that reduce business net profit from deductions that are taken elsewhere on the individual return.

WHAT ABOUT A FORM 2553 ELECTION FOR 2026?

For a calendar-year entity seeking an S election effective January 1, 2026, the normal timely-election deadline was March 16, 2026 (because March 15 fell on a Sunday). Late-election relief may be available when the requirements are met. That is different from the March 15, 2027 deadline for filing the 2026 Form 1120-S.

State Taxes Are a Separate Layer—and “No Income Tax” Is Not the Same as “No Business Tax”

Federal classification does not tell you the full cost of operating an LLC. States can impose annual LLC taxes, gross-receipts taxes, franchise taxes, fees, entity-level elections and individual income taxes. A state with no broad individual income tax can still impose meaningful business taxes.

Three current examples show why a generic state list can become misleading:

  • California: The 2026 annual LLC tax is $800 for LLCs subject to the state's rules, with an additional LLC fee at higher California-source income levels.
  • Delaware: Current Division of Corporations guidance lists a $300 annual tax for LLCs, LPs and GPs, due on or before June 1.
  • Washington: Most businesses are subject to the B&O tax, which is measured on gross receipts rather than net profit.

For that reason, choosing a tax classification should not be separated from the state where the business actually operates. The federal guide on LLC costs and ongoing fees can help identify recurring costs, but state tax advice should ultimately be checked against the relevant state tax agency.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Questions That Do Not Fit Into a One-Line Tax Rule

Classification and ownership

01 Does an LLC pay federal income tax itself?

By default, no. Single-member LLCs (disregarded entities) and multi-member LLCs (partnerships) are pass-through entities. The income flows through to the owners' tax returns. Only LLCs that elect C-corporation status pay entity-level federal income tax.

02 Is every single-member LLC taxed on Schedule C?

No. While Schedule C is used for an operating trade or business, single-member LLCs holding rental real estate report on Schedule E, and farming LLCs report on Schedule F.

03 Is all partnership LLC income subject to self-employment tax?

No. Self-employment tax applies to active trade or business income, but passive rental income, capital gains, and certain limited partner shares are generally exempt from SE tax.

Election and deduction questions

04 Can a non-U.S. owner elect S-corporation status?

No. The IRS prohibits non-resident aliens from owning shares in an S corporation. Non-U.S. founders must remain as a pass-through partnership/disregarded entity or elect C-corporation tax status.

05 Does the 20% QBI deduction reduce self-employment tax?

No. The Section 199A QBI deduction reduces your federal taxable income (AGI adjustment), but it does not reduce your net self-employment earnings for calculating SE tax.

06 What should I review before changing my LLC's tax classification?

Before filing Form 2553 (S-Corp) or Form 8832 (C-Corp), evaluate your projected net profit, reasonable W-2 salary requirements, payroll administration software costs, state franchise/annual tax rules, and eligibility criteria.

BOTTOM LINE
The most useful way to understand LLC taxes is to separate state-law entity from federal tax classification. One owner usually starts as a disregarded entity; multiple owners usually start as a partnership; eligible LLCs can elect S-corp treatment; and an LLC can elect C-corp taxation. From there, the correct answer depends on profit, ownership, compensation, QBI, state taxes and the type of income—not on a single "LLC tax rate."
