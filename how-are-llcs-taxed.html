How Are LLCs Taxed? (2026 Federal & State Tax Guide)

Pass-Through Tax Treatment, S-Corp & C-Corp Elections, QBI & Self-Employment Tax Breakdown

An LLC is a legal structure created under state law, but “LLC” is not a federal income-tax classification. For federal tax purposes, an LLC may be treated as a disregarded entity, partnership, C corporation, or—if eligible and properly elected—an S corporation. The classification affects tax returns, payroll requirements, self-employment tax, and other federal tax rules.

For most owners, the first question is not “What tax rate does an LLC pay?” but which federal tax classification applies to the LLC? A single-member LLC generally defaults to disregarded-entity treatment, while a multi-member LLC generally defaults to partnership treatment. An eligible LLC can elect corporate tax treatment, including S-corporation status.

⚠️ 2026 Tax Year Does Not Mean “File the Return in 2026”

Most calendar-year returns reporting 2026 income are filed in 2027. Estimated tax payments for 2026 are generally made during 2026 and January 2027, while the related federal income-tax returns are generally due in 2027.

Start Here: Which Federal Tax Treatment Applies?

The IRS classification rules generally start with the number of owners. A domestic LLC with one owner is generally disregarded for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. A domestic LLC with two or more members is generally treated as a partnership unless it elects corporate treatment. An eligible LLC can elect S-corporation status by filing Form 2553. For a deeper explanation of the legal structure itself, see the LLC Guide hub.

LEGAL ENTITY AND TAX CLASSIFICATION ARE DIFFERENT LAYERS

An LLC that elects corporate taxation does not automatically become a corporation under state law. The federal election changes how the IRS taxes the entity; it does not change the company's state-law formation documents.

Single-Member LLC: What the Default Really Means

A single-member LLC owned by an individual is generally disregarded for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. The LLC's income and deductions are therefore reported on the owner's federal return—commonly Schedule C for an operating trade or business, although Schedule E or Schedule F can apply in other situations.

"Disregarded" does not mean tax-free. If the owner operates a trade or business, net earnings from self-employment are generally subject to self-employment tax. The headline self-employment tax rate is 15.3%, but it is not simply 15.3% of every dollar of Schedule C profit. The calculation generally starts with 92.35% of net earnings, the 12.4% Social Security component is subject to the annual wage base, and Medicare tax rules continue above that wage base.

The purpose is to illustrate the interaction among self-employment tax, the deductible half of self-employment tax, the 2026 standard deduction, and the QBI limitation—not to calculate the owner's final federal income-tax bill.

Multi-Member LLC: Pass-Through Does Not Mean “No Return”

A domestic multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate treatment. The partnership generally files Form 1065 and provides each member with a Schedule K-1. The partnership generally does not pay federal income tax itself; instead, tax items pass through to the members and are reported on their individual returns.

S-Corp Election: What Changes—and What Does Not

An LLC that is eligible for S-corporation treatment can file Form 2553. The election generally preserves pass-through federal income-tax treatment while adding corporate payroll rules. A shareholder who performs services for the S corporation can be treated as an employee, and the IRS generally requires reasonable compensation before non-wage distributions are used as a substitute for pay for those services.

This can create potential payroll-tax savings in some profitable owner-operated businesses, but there is no universal profit level at which an S-corp election automatically makes sense. The result depends on reasonable compensation, payroll and tax-preparation costs, state taxes, other wages, retirement and health-benefit choices, QBI limitations, and the stability of the business's profit.

QUESTION BUSINESS OWNERS REPEATEDLY ASK
“At what income does an S-corp actually start making sense?”
✓
There is no IRS break-even number. A $50,000 or $75,000 rule of thumb can be misleading because two owners with the same business profit can have different reasonable salaries, state taxes, and compliance costs. A meaningful comparison should consider projected payroll taxes, reasonable compensation, QBI effects, and annual administrative costs based on the owner's specific circumstances.

Eligibility Is Not Automatic

An LLC cannot choose S-corp status simply because the owner wants potential payroll-tax savings. The entity must meet the federal S-corporation eligibility requirements, including being a domestic entity, having no more than 100 shareholders, having only eligible shareholders, having no nonresident-alien shareholders, and having only one class of stock for federal tax purposes. Form 2553 is generally used to make the S-corporation election.

A SALARY PERCENTAGE IS NOT AN IRS SAFE HARBOR

The tax code does not provide a fixed “60/40” split or another universal reasonable-compensation percentage. The IRS and courts can consider facts and circumstances such as the owner's duties, experience, time devoted to the business, comparable compensation, and how payments are structured.

C-Corp Treatment: A Different Taxpayer Enters the Picture

An LLC can elect to be treated as a corporation for federal tax purposes, commonly by filing Form 8832. If the LLC is taxed as a C corporation, it generally files Form 1120 and is subject to the federal corporate income-tax regime. The federal corporate income-tax rate is currently 21%.

The key difference is that C-corporation income does not simply pass through to the owners in the same way as default LLC or S-corp income. If after-tax corporate earnings are later distributed as dividends, shareholders may face a second layer of federal tax. Qualified dividends can receive preferential federal tax rates when the applicable requirements are met, but the actual shareholder tax result depends on income, holding period, and other factors.

There is also an important legal distinction: electing C-corp tax treatment does not convert an LLC into a state-law corporation. If a business is raising institutional capital or needs a conventional corporate equity structure, the legal-entity decision should be evaluated separately from the federal tax election rather than assuming Form 8832 accomplishes both.

The 2026 QBI Deduction: Permanent, but Still Conditional

The Section 199A qualified business income (QBI) deduction is now permanent. Eligible owners of sole proprietorships, partnerships, and S corporations can generally deduct up to 20% of qualified business income, subject to taxable-income limits and, at higher income levels, wage/property and specified-service-business rules. C-corporation income is not QBI.

The familiar 20% figure is a maximum framework, not a guarantee that every eligible owner will receive a deduction equal to 20% of business profit.

Does QBI Make the S-Corp Election Pointless?

No. QBI and S-corporation payroll rules address different tax questions. An S-corp shareholder's W-2 wages generally are not QBI, while eligible pass-through business income may qualify for the Section 199A deduction.

Changing the amount of reasonable compensation can therefore affect both payroll taxes and the QBI calculation. This is one reason a one-line formula such as “an S-corp saves X%” can be misleading. See our broader LLC Tax Benefits & Deductions guide for a detailed comparison of these interactions.

Self-Employment Tax and Deductions: Keep the Buckets Separate

For 2026, the Social Security portion of self-employment tax is subject to a $184,500 taxable maximum. Medicare tax has no comparable earnings cap, and the additional 0.9% Medicare tax can apply above the applicable income thresholds.

A person who also earns W-2 wages needs to consider those wages when determining how much of the annual Social Security wage base remains available for self-employment income.

Tax deductions are sometimes described as though they all reduce the same amount. They do not. A more useful approach is to separate expenses that reduce business net profit from deductions that are taken elsewhere on the individual return.

Three 2026 Numbers That Changed

  • Business mileage: 772.5¢ per mile for January 1–June 30, 2026, and 75¢ per mile for July 1–December 31, 2026.
  • 401(k) elective deferral: $24,500 for 2026, before applicable catch-up contributions.
  • Defined-contribution / SEP dollar ceiling: $72,000 for 2026, subject to applicable compensation, contribution, and plan rules.

A self-employed person's SEP contribution is not simply “25% of Schedule C profit.” The self-employed calculation adjusts compensation for self-employment tax and applies the applicable plan rules. Vehicle, home-office, health-insurance, and retirement deductions also have their own qualification, calculation, and substantiation requirements.

If you receive or make payments that require contractor information returns, note that the federal reporting threshold for many Form 1099-NEC payments increased to $2,000 for payments made after December 31, 2025. Other reporting rules and exceptions can apply.

WHAT ABOUT A FORM 2553 ELECTION FOR 2026?

For a calendar-year entity seeking an S-corporation election effective January 1, 2026, the normal timely-election deadline was March 16, 2026, because March 15 fell on a Sunday. Late-election relief may be available when the applicable requirements are met. That deadline is separate from the March 15, 2027 filing deadline for a calendar-year LLC's 2026 Form 1120-S.

State Taxes Are a Separate Layer—and “No Income Tax” Is Not the Same as “No Business Tax”

Federal tax classification does not tell you the full cost of operating an LLC. States can impose annual LLC taxes, gross-receipts taxes, franchise taxes, business fees, entity-level taxes, and individual income taxes. A state with no broad individual income tax can still impose meaningful taxes on businesses.

Three current examples show why a generic state tax list can become misleading:

  • California: LLCs subject to California's rules generally owe an $800 annual tax. An additional LLC fee applies when California-source income reaches $250,000 or more, with the amount increasing at higher income levels.
  • Delaware: Delaware LLCs are currently subject to a $300 annual tax, due on or before June 1. Delaware LLCs do not file an annual report.
  • Washington: Washington's Business & Occupation (B&O) tax is generally measured on gross receipts rather than net profit. The applicable rate depends on the business activity and classification.

For that reason, federal tax classification should not be evaluated separately from the state where the business actually operates. The federal guide on LLC costs and ongoing fees can help identify recurring costs, but state tax rules should ultimately be checked with the relevant state tax agency.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Questions That Do Not Fit Into a One-Line Tax Rule

Classification and ownership

01 Do all LLCs pay federal income tax at the entity level?

No. A single-member LLC that is disregarded for federal income-tax purposes generally reports its business activity on the owner's return, while a partnership generally passes its income, deductions, gains, losses, and credits through to its members. An LLC taxed as an S corporation generally also passes these items through to its shareholders. A C corporation generally pays federal income tax at the entity level.

02 Does every single-member LLC file Schedule C?

No. Schedule C is commonly used when the LLC operates a trade or business. Other activities can be reported differently—for example, rental real estate may generally be reported on Schedule E, while farming income may be reported on Schedule F when the applicable requirements are met.

03 Does every type of LLC income face self-employment tax?

No. Self-employment tax generally applies to net earnings from a trade or business, but different rules can apply to passive rental income, capital gains, and certain partnership interests. The member's role and the character of the income can matter.

Election and deduction questions

04 Can a non-resident alien own an S corporation?

Generally, no. Federal S-corporation rules prohibit nonresident-alien shareholders. An LLC with a nonresident-alien owner therefore cannot simply elect S-corporation status while that owner remains a shareholder. Other federal tax classifications may be available depending on the LLC's ownership and circumstances.

05 Does the QBI deduction reduce self-employment tax?

No. The Section 199A QBI deduction is a deduction used in determining taxable income; it does not reduce net earnings from self-employment or the self-employment tax calculation.

06 Should I file Form 2553 or Form 8832 without comparing the numbers first?

Not necessarily. Before making an election, consider projected net profit, reasonable compensation where applicable, payroll and tax-preparation costs, state franchise or annual taxes, QBI effects, eligibility requirements, and the administrative requirements that come with the selected tax treatment.

BOTTOM LINE
The most useful way to understand LLC taxes is to separate the state-law entity from its federal tax classification. One owner usually starts as a disregarded entity; multiple owners usually start as a partnership; an eligible LLC can elect S-corporation treatment; and an LLC can elect corporate taxation. From there, the correct result depends on profit, ownership, compensation, QBI, state taxes, and the type of income—not on a single “LLC tax rate.”
