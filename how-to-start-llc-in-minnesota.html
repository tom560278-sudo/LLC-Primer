How to Start an LLC in Minnesota in 2026

Last reviewed: October 2026  ·  Primary sources: Minnesota Secretary of State, Minnesota Department of Revenue

Minnesota charges $155 to file Articles of Organization online or $135 by mail. The state does not charge a fee for the required annual renewal, so the Secretary of State portion of ongoing LLC maintenance can be $0 per year when the renewal is filed on time.

To start an LLC in Minnesota, you file Articles of Organization with the Minnesota Secretary of State, maintain a Minnesota registered office, obtain an EIN when required, and keep up with state and federal tax obligations. Minnesota also has a Minimum Fee that can apply to LLCs taxed as partnerships when their Minnesota property, payroll, and sales or receipts exceed the annual threshold.

For tax year 2026, the Minnesota Minimum Fee begins when the applicable Minnesota property, payroll, and sales or receipts total reaches $1,280,000. The 2026 fee ranges from $260 to $12,830, depending on the total. A single-member LLC that reports its income directly on the owner's individual return is not subject to the Minimum Fee.

Minnesota's $0 Annual Renewal

Minnesota does not charge a filing fee for the annual renewal of an active domestic LLC. The renewal is required once every calendar year, and filing it keeps the LLC's Secretary of State record current. The $0 renewal does not mean an LLC has no other annual obligations. Depending on the business, you may still have federal income-tax filings, Minnesota tax filings, local licenses, sales-tax obligations, payroll filings, or other regulatory requirements. For LLCs taxed as partnerships, Minnesota's Minimum Fee may also apply when the business exceeds the state's annual property, payroll, and sales-or-receipts threshold.

1. Check and Choose Your Minnesota LLC Name

Your Minnesota LLC name must comply with Minnesota's naming rules and be distinguishable from existing business names on file with the Secretary of State. Before filing, search the Minnesota business database to see whether the name is available. Your legal name must also contain an accepted LLC designator.

2. Registered Office and Agent

Minnesota has an important distinction between a registered office and a registered agent.

A domestic Minnesota LLC must maintain a registered office address in Minnesota. The address must be a physical street address or qualifying rural route address; a P.O. box by itself is not acceptable.

A registered agent is not required for a domestic Minnesota LLC. If you choose to designate one, the agent must meet Minnesota's requirements and be located at the registered office.

Registered Office vs. Registered Agent
  • Registered Office: The registered office is the Minnesota address maintained by the LLC for official correspondence and service-related purposes.
  • Registered Agent: A registered agent is a person or qualifying business entity designated to receive service of process for the LLC. Minnesota's domestic LLC formation documents state that an agent is not required.

Should You Use a Professional Registered Office or Agent Service? A professional registered agent service can be useful if you do not want to use your home or office address for the LLC's public state record, or if you want help receiving and forwarding official documents. The decision is optional for a domestic Minnesota LLC. Compare the service's current price, renewal cost, address coverage, mail handling, and document-forwarding terms before purchasing.

Online vs. Mail Filing: The online filing costs $20 more than filing by mail. The trade-off is that online filing is processed through Minnesota's expedited filing system. 4. Create an Operating or Member Control Agreement

Minnesota does not require every LLC to file an operating agreement with the Secretary of State.

For an LLC with multiple owners, however, a written agreement can document how the company will operate and how ownership decisions will be handled. Minnesota law also recognizes member control agreements, which can address governance and ownership arrangements among members.

What Your Agreement Can Cover:

  • Ownership percentages
  • Member contributions
  • Voting rights
  • Management responsibilities
  • Profit and loss allocations
  • Distribution rules
  • Admission of new members
  • Transfer of membership interests
  • Buyout procedures
  • What happens when a member leaves
  • Dissolution procedures
  • Dispute-resolution procedures

Single-Member vs. Multi-Member LLCs: A single-member LLC may use a simpler agreement documenting the owner's control and separation between the individual and the business. A multi-member LLC generally benefits from a more detailed agreement because disagreements over ownership, management, distributions, or transfers can become more complicated.

Minnesota's LLC statute contains specific rules concerning charging orders and membership interests. Because those rules can have legal consequences, owners with significant assets or multiple members should consider having a Minnesota attorney review the agreement.

5. Get Your EIN

An Employer Identification Number (EIN) is the federal tax identification number issued by the IRS. The IRS provides EINs free of charge. Do not pay a third party simply to obtain an EIN unless the service is bundled into another service you intentionally purchase.

An EIN may be needed for:

  • Hiring employees
  • Opening a business bank account
  • Filing certain federal tax returns
  • Filing certain Minnesota tax returns
  • Operating a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership

6. Open a Business Bank Account

After forming your LLC, establish a separate financial account for business activity. Keeping business and personal finances separate creates cleaner accounting records and helps demonstrate that the LLC is operated as a separate business entity. Compare your options for a dedicated business bank account.

Banks commonly ask for:

  • Approved Articles of Organization
  • EIN
  • Operating or Member Control Agreement
  • Government-issued identification

Bank requirements vary, so confirm the exact documentation with the financial institution before opening the account.

7. Minnesota LLC Taxes — Including the 2026 Minimum Fee

Minnesota LLC taxation depends on the LLC's federal tax classification and the business's circumstances. A single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects different treatment. A multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership unless it makes another election. See how LLCs are taxed for a general overview.

The 2026 Minnesota Minimum Fee

The Minnesota Minimum Fee is particularly important for LLCs that file as partnerships. According to the Minnesota Department of Revenue, an LLC with two or more members that files as a partnership can be subject to the Minimum Fee when its Minnesota property, payroll, and sales or receipts exceed the applicable threshold. A single-member LLC that reports its income and other information only on the owner's individual Minnesota return is not subject to the Minimum Fee.

The thresholds and amounts are adjusted annually for inflation.

Who Needs to Pay It? The Minimum Fee generally matters when an LLC is filing as a partnership and has Minnesota property, payroll, and sales or receipts above the applicable threshold. The Minimum Fee is determined through the appropriate Minnesota tax return, such as Form M3 for partnerships. If your business is approaching the threshold, review the current Minnesota Department of Revenue instructions.

S-Corporation and Corporate Tax Elections

An LLC can potentially elect a different federal tax classification, including S-corporation treatment, if it meets the applicable requirements. The tax result depends on the owner's circumstances, profit level, payroll, deductions, federal rules, Minnesota rules, and other factors. There is no universal tax advantage to making an S-corporation election. Owners considering an election should model the federal and Minnesota consequences with a qualified tax professional before changing their tax classification.

9. Annual Renewal and Compliance Requirements

Every Minnesota LLC governed under Chapter 322C must file an Annual Renewal once every calendar year. The Minnesota Secretary of State does not charge a filing fee for the annual renewal when the LLC is active and in good standing.

The Annual Renewal is only one part of business compliance. Filing it does not replace tax returns or other filings that may apply.

Missing the Annual Renewal: Minnesota states that an LLC dissolved for failure to file its annual renewal can be retroactively reinstated by filing the current renewal and paying the applicable reinstatement fee. The current Minnesota Secretary of State fee schedule lists domestic LLC annual reinstatement at $65 by mail and $85 online. Do not assume that reinstatement is the only consequence of a missed renewal. A dissolved business may also need to address banking, contracts, licensing, tax, and other practical issues.

11. Common Mistakes When Starting a Minnesota LLC

Mistake 1: Assuming the $0 Annual Renewal Means Zero Annual Compliance Cost — Minnesota's annual renewal is free, but an LLC may still have tax filings, licenses, permits, payroll obligations, insurance costs, or other expenses. Treat the $0 figure as the Secretary of State annual renewal fee, not as a guarantee that your business has no ongoing costs.

Mistake 2: Using an Outdated Minimum Fee Threshold — Minnesota adjusts its Minimum Fee annually. For 2026, the threshold begins at $1,280,000, with higher brackets reaching a maximum listed Minimum Fee of $12,830. Do not use an older $970,000 or $1.25 million threshold when calculating a 2026 obligation.

Mistake 3: Assuming Every Minnesota LLC Needs a Registered Agent — Minnesota requires a registered office, but a registered agent is not required for a domestic LLC. If you designate an agent, make sure the agent meets Minnesota's requirements.

Mistake 4: Using Only a P.O. Box for the Registered Office — A P.O. box by itself is not acceptable for the Minnesota registered office. Use a qualifying Minnesota physical address.

Mistake 5: Mixing Personal and Business Finances — Use a dedicated business account and maintain separate records for LLC transactions. Good recordkeeping does not guarantee liability protection, but it helps maintain a clear separation between the business and its owners.

Mistake 6: Treating an Operating Agreement as a State Filing — Minnesota does not require you to file your operating agreement with the Secretary of State. The agreement is an internal business document. Keep the signed version with your company records.

Mistake 7: Assuming a Tax Election Automatically Saves Money — An S-corporation election or other tax classification can have benefits in some circumstances and additional costs or tax consequences in others. Run the numbers for your specific business before making the election.
