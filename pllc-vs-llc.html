There is no reliable national rule saying that every licensed professional must form a PLLC. States use different professional-entity models, and the rules can also vary by profession.

The key question is not simply "Does my state allow PLLCs?" It is: What entity may my profession use in this state, what licensing and ownership conditions apply, and what personal liability remains despite the entity structure?

PLLC vs LLC: What the Distinction Actually Means

An LLC is a state-law business entity. A PLLC, where the term is used, is generally a professional-services version or status within a state's LLC framework. States do not implement professional LLCs uniformly.

Malpractice Liability: Separate Your Own Acts from Entity Debts

One of the most important distinctions in professional-entity planning is the difference between entity-level liability and personal professional liability. Forming an LLC or PLLC does not automatically eliminate a professional's personal responsibility for their own negligent or wrongful professional acts.

Practical Liability Question: If my state lets me use a normal LLC, does that make my personal assets safe from malpractice claims? No. The permitted entity and the scope of professional-liability protection are separate questions.

How to Choose the Permitted Entity

1
Identify your profession and licensing authority — Start with the license you use to provide professional services.
2
Check the state's entity statute — Determine whether the state provides an explicit PLLC, professional-service LLC, ordinary LLC subject to professional restrictions, RPC, professional corporation, professional association, or another profession-specific entity.
3
Check your profession's own rules — Professional licensing law may impose additional requirements involving ownership, management, professional control, naming, board approval, multidisciplinary ownership, permitted services, or loss of licensure.
4
Analyze liability separately — Do not assume that the entity name answers the malpractice question. The important distinction is between protection from certain entity obligations and personal responsibility for your own professional conduct.
5
Review tax and insurance consequences — Once the permitted entity is identified, evaluate federal tax classification, state tax treatment, payroll/self-employment tax, insurance, and other compliance obligations.

PLLC Formation Requirements Vary by State

Licensing-board approval is not universal. Some states require a certificate, approval, or verification from a professional licensing body before or during formation. That is not a nationwide rule.

Naming rules are not universal either. A professional LLC does not necessarily have to end in "PLLC." The permitted ending depends on state law and can include: Professional Limited Liability Company, PLLC, P.L.L.C., Limited Liability Company, LLC, or other statutory designations.

Ownership and management rules can be restrictive. There is no universal rule that every owner must hold the same professional license. Depending on the state and profession, statutes may require all owners to be licensed, permit specified combinations of licensed professionals, or impose other ownership restrictions.

How a PLLC Is Taxed Federally in 2026

The IRS does not create a separate federal tax classification simply because a state calls an entity a PLLC. Federal taxation generally follows the same LLC classification framework. See our LLC vs S-Corp guide for additional information on S-corporation elections.

Federal SituationGeneral Treatment
Single-member LLC/PLLCGenerally disregarded for federal income-tax purposes unless corporate election made
Multi-member LLC/PLLCGenerally classified as partnership unless eligible corporate election made
Corporate electionAn eligible LLC can elect corporate treatment under federal tax rules
S corporation electionAn eligible entity may elect S-corp treatment if all applicable requirements satisfied
No Universal IRS S-Corp Threshold: There is no IRS rule saying that a professional who earns $80,000 or another specific profit amount should automatically elect S-corporation taxation. The decision depends on reasonable compensation, payroll costs, state taxes, administrative costs, retirement planning, and other factors.

PLLC vs LLC Frequently Asked Questions

01 What is the main difference between a PLLC and an LLC?

A PLLC or professional LLC is a state-law structure designed for qualifying professional services. The exact definition, ownership requirements and filing process vary by jurisdiction. It is not a separate federal tax classification.

02 How many states allow PLLCs?

A single number is misleading. Some states expressly authorize PLLCs, while others allow professional services through ordinary LLCs, profession-specific LLC structures or specialized professional entities. LLC Primer uses a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction model rather than presenting one national PLLC count.

03 Can a doctor, lawyer or CPA always form a PLLC?

No. The permitted entity depends on the state and profession. A profession may be permitted to use a PLLC in one state and required or limited to another professional entity in another state.

04 Can a professional use an ordinary LLC?

Sometimes. Several jurisdictions permit certain professional services through an ordinary LLC subject to professional licensing restrictions. Other states or professions require a specialized professional entity.

05 Does a PLLC name always have to contain 'PLLC'?

No. Naming rules vary by state. Some jurisdictions permit professional LLCs to use ordinary LLC endings or multiple statutory variations.

06 Is licensing-board approval always required before filing?

No. Some states require professional licensing-body certification or approval, while others use different verification procedures or do not require advance approval.

07 Does a standard LLC protect a professional from their own malpractice?

Do not assume so. The entity may protect against certain business liabilities, but professional licensing and malpractice rules can preserve personal responsibility for a professional's own conduct.

08 Does a PLLC protect me from another member's malpractice?

The entity may provide protection in some circumstances, but participation, supervision, agency principles and profession-specific laws can affect the result.

09 Is malpractice insurance required for every PLLC?

No. Insurance requirements vary by profession and jurisdiction. Some practices may have specific insurance or financial-responsibility requirements, while others may purchase coverage as a risk-management measure.

10 Is a PLLC taxed differently from a standard LLC by the IRS?

Not simply because it is called a PLLC. The IRS generally applies the same LLC classification framework based on ownership and valid tax elections.
