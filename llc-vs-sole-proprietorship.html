LLC vs Sole Proprietorship (2026)

When you're comparing an LLC vs. sole proprietorship, the key questions are how the business is legally structured, what personal liability protection is available, how the business is taxed, and what ongoing costs and compliance obligations apply.


A sole proprietorship is generally the simplest business structure to operate because there is no separate legal entity to create. An LLC (Limited Liability Company) is a state-created business entity that can provide a legal separation between the business and its owners when properly formed and maintained.


The tax comparison also requires an important distinction: forming an LLC does not automatically reduce federal income or self-employment taxes. A single-member LLC is generally taxed by the IRS as a disregarded entity by default, much like a sole proprietorship. An LLC can, however, elect a different federal tax classification, including S-corporation status, if it meets the applicable requirements.

The Core Legal Difference

A sole proprietorship generally does not create a separate legal entity from its owner. The business activity and the owner are legally connected, which means the owner can generally be personally responsible for the business's debts and liabilities.

An LLC (Limited Liability Company) is a separate legal entity created under state law. The LLC can own property, enter contracts, maintain its own accounts, and incur business obligations separately from its members. That separation can provide important personal liability protection, but it is not unlimited or automatic. Personal guarantees, the owner's own wrongful conduct, certain taxes and obligations, and failures to maintain the entity can result in personal liability.

Formation Requirements

Sole Proprietorship: Generally, no entity-formation filing is required to begin operating as a sole proprietor. However, a business license, tax registration, assumed-name/DBA registration, or other local or state registration may still be required depending on the business.

LLC: An LLC is generally created by filing Articles of Organization, a Certificate of Formation, or a similar document with the state. Filing fees vary substantially by state. See our how to start an LLC guide for state-by-state instructions.

Important: Using your own name for a business does not automatically create an LLC. A person operating a business without forming a separate entity will generally operate as a sole proprietor unless another structure has been established. Many sole proprietors also register a DBA (Doing Business As) when they want to operate under a trade name. A DBA generally does not create a separate legal entity or provide LLC-style liability protection.

How Each Structure Is Taxed

A single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity for federal income-tax purposes unless the owner makes an election to have the LLC taxed as a corporation. This means its business income is generally reported on the owner's federal tax return in much the same way as a sole proprietorship.

Sole Proprietorship Tax: Schedule C and Self-Employment Tax

A sole proprietor generally reports business income and expenses on Schedule C (Form 1040). Net earnings from self-employment may also be subject to self-employment tax. The commonly cited 15.3% self-employment tax rate consists of Social Security and Medicare taxes. However, the actual tax calculation is more complicated than simply multiplying all business profit by 15.3%. For 2026, the Social Security portion is subject to the applicable annual wage base ($184,500), while the Medicare portion generally has no Social Security-style wage cap.

LLC Default Tax: Similar to a Sole Proprietorship

Forming a single-member LLC does not automatically change its federal income-tax classification. Unless an election is made, the IRS generally treats a single-member LLC as a disregarded entity. The owner typically reports the LLC's business activity on the owner's federal return, generally including Schedule C when applicable. For more detail, see our guide on how LLCs are taxed.

The S-Corp Election: A Different Tax Treatment

An eligible LLC can elect to be taxed as an S corporation by filing Form 2553 with the IRS. Under S-corporation taxation, an owner who performs services for the business generally must receive reasonable compensation as wages before taking distributions. However, an S-corp election does not automatically produce tax savings. See our LLC vs. S-Corp guide and LLC vs. S-Corp Tax Calculator for additional information.

What Liability Protection Actually Means in Practice

The primary legal difference between a sole proprietorship and an LLC is the potential separation between the business and the owner's personal assets. As a sole proprietor, the business generally has no separate legal identity from its owner. An LLC generally provides a liability shield for its members. Business debts and liabilities are generally obligations of the LLC rather than automatically becoming personal obligations of its members.

When LLC Protection Can Fail

Common issues that can create personal exposure include:

  • Mixing personal and business finances
  • Using the LLC as a personal alter ego rather than maintaining separation
  • Personally guaranteeing a business loan, lease, or other obligation
  • Fraud or intentional misconduct
  • Failing to follow important state requirements
  • Signing contracts in a personal capacity rather than on behalf of the LLC

Which One May Be Right for You?

There is no single income number that determines when someone must form an LLC. Not sure where to start? Try our Business Structure Quiz for an educational comparison.

Consider a Sole Proprietorship If

  • You're testing a business idea with low or unpredictable revenue
  • The business has relatively limited liability exposure
  • You want minimal entity-level administration

Consider Forming an LLC If

  • Your business creates meaningful liability exposure
  • You want a formal legal entity recognized by the state
  • You have business assets or significant revenue to separate
  • You're bringing in another owner or co-founder
  • You're considering different tax treatment in the future
  • You want a structure that can grow with the business

How to Convert a Sole Proprietorship to an LLC

Moving from a sole proprietorship to an LLC generally involves forming the new entity and then transferring the business activity to it. The exact process varies by state and by business type.

1
Form the LLC with your state

Choose an available business name, prepare the required formation document, appoint a registered agent, and submit the filing to the appropriate state agency. See our guide on how to start an LLC for state-by-step instructions.

2
Appoint a registered agent

An LLC generally needs a registered agent in its formation state. Compare providers in our LLC formation services guide.

3
Get an EIN when required

An EIN is issued by the IRS at no charge. See our EIN guide for the application process.

4
Open a dedicated business bank account

Maintain a separate account for the LLC's business activity. See our business bank account guide.

5
Transfer business activity and update contracts

Update invoices, contracts, vendor relationships, licenses, permits, and other records to reflect the new LLC where appropriate.

What Happens After: A sole proprietorship generally does not require a formal dissolution filing because it is not a separate entity. However, the owner should properly account for business income earned before and after the LLC begins operating and address any required final tax filings, licenses, DBAs, contracts, and registrations.

Key Takeaway: A sole proprietorship is generally simpler and less expensive to establish, but it does not create the same separate legal entity as an LLC. An LLC can provide a liability shield when it is properly formed and maintained, but the protection is not absolute. Forming an LLC also does not automatically change federal tax treatment. A single-member LLC generally receives the same default federal tax treatment as a sole proprietorship unless the owner makes an eligible tax election.

Frequently Asked Questions

01 Is an LLC taxed differently than a sole proprietorship?

Not necessarily. A single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity for federal income-tax purposes unless the owner elects another classification. As a result, its federal tax treatment can be similar to that of a sole proprietorship. An LLC may be eligible to elect S-corporation taxation, which can change how employment taxes apply to owner compensation. However, the potential benefit depends on the owner's circumstances and the additional administrative costs involved.

02 Is a single-member LLC the same as a sole proprietorship for tax purposes?

For federal income-tax purposes, a single-member LLC is generally taxed like a sole proprietorship by default. Legally, however, they are different structures. A sole proprietorship generally does not create a separate legal entity, while an LLC does. That distinction is important because the LLC can provide a liability shield and may be eligible for different federal tax elections.

03 Does a sole proprietor need an EIN?

Not always. A sole proprietor without employees may generally use the owner's Social Security number for federal tax purposes. However, there are circumstances in which an EIN is required, and some owners obtain one voluntarily for business or banking purposes. An EIN is issued by the IRS for free.

04 Can a sole proprietor be sued personally?

Yes. Because a sole proprietorship generally does not create a separate legal entity, the owner can be personally responsible for business liabilities and judgments, subject to applicable law and exemptions.

05 Can an LLC owner still be personally liable?

Yes. An LLC's liability protection is not absolute. Personal guarantees, personal wrongdoing, certain tax obligations, and other circumstances can create personal liability. The exact rules governing an LLC's liability shield vary by state.

06 Can a sole proprietorship have employees?

Yes. A sole proprietor can hire employees and is responsible for applicable employment, payroll, tax, insurance, and workplace requirements. Hiring employees does not automatically require an LLC, although increased liability and administrative complexity may make entity formation worth considering.

07 How much does it cost to go from a sole proprietorship to an LLC?

There is no single nationwide cost. You generally need to pay the applicable state LLC formation fee. Depending on the state and business, you may also have recurring annual or biennial fees, franchise taxes, registered-agent costs, licenses, permits, DBA fees, or professional service costs. Use our LLC Cost Guide for state-specific information.

08 What is the income threshold to switch from a sole proprietorship to an LLC?

There is no federal income threshold that requires a business owner to form an LLC. Liability exposure, business activity, contracts, employees, assets, ownership, state requirements, and tax considerations can all influence the decision.

09 How do I know which state to form my LLC in?

For many small businesses, the practical starting point is the state where the business is actually operated. Forming in another state can create additional registration, registered-agent, annual-report, and tax obligations. See our guide to the best state to form an LLC for a more detailed comparison.
