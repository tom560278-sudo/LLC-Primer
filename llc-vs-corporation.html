LLC vs Corporation (2026): How to Choose

The LLC vs. corporation decision depends on more than taxes. Ownership structure, fundraising plans, governance requirements, liability protection, administrative work, state requirements, and the way owners expect to receive profits can all affect which structure is appropriate.


For many closely held small businesses, an LLC provides a flexible ownership and management structure with several available federal tax classifications. A corporation can be particularly relevant when a business expects to issue stock, bring in institutional investors, establish certain equity compensation plans, or pursue a structure commonly used by venture-backed companies.


There is also an important distinction between legal entity type and federal tax classification. An LLC is a state-law business entity. For federal tax purposes, an LLC may be treated as a disregarded entity, partnership, C corporation, or, if eligible, S corporation.

What They Actually Are: Structure and Ownership

LLC: Flexible Ownership and Management

An LLC is owned by members. Members can generally hold different ownership percentages, subject to the LLC's governing documents and state law. An LLC can be member-managed or manager-managed. These arrangements are generally established through an operating agreement. An LLC can generally have one or multiple members, and members can include individuals and certain business entities. For more information, see our guide on multi-member LLCs and what an LLC is.

Corporation: Shareholders, Directors, and Officers

A corporation is owned by shareholders, who hold shares of stock. Corporate governance generally involves shareholders (who vote on matters reserved for shareholders), a Board of Directors (which oversees the corporation's affairs), Officers (who handle day-to-day management), Bylaws (which provide internal governance rules), and Corporate Records.

C-Corp vs S-Corp: Tax Classifications, Not Two Different Entity Types

When people say "corporation," they often mean a C corporation, which is the standard federal corporate tax classification. An S corporation is a federal tax election available to eligible corporations and certain eligible LLCs. An eligible LLC can generally elect corporate tax treatment and, if it meets the requirements, make an S-corporation election. See our LLC vs. S-Corp guide for a separate comparison.

C-Corporation: Corporate-Level Tax and Dividends

A traditional C corporation generally pays federal income tax at the applicable corporate rate. Under current federal law, the general corporate income-tax rate is 21%. If after-tax corporate profits are distributed as dividends, shareholders may also owe tax on those dividends. This is commonly described as double taxation.

Management and Compliance: What Each Structure Requires

LLC Compliance: Flexible, but Not Requirement-Free

Many states require LLCs to file periodic reports or statements. Use our LLC Annual Report Tracker to review state-specific deadlines. LLCs generally must also maintain a registered agent and keep appropriate financial, ownership, tax, and compliance records.

Corporation Compliance: More Formal Governance

Corporations commonly have state reporting obligations, formal procedures for shareholder actions, board governance requirements, bylaws and corporate records, stock records, and must file federal corporate tax returns (Form 1120 for C-corp; Form 1120-S for S-corp).

Formation and Ongoing Costs: Side by Side

Cost should be considered alongside the business's needs rather than used as the only deciding factor. For state-specific LLC costs, see how much an LLC costs. There is no reliable nationwide five-year cost that applies to every LLC or corporation.

When a Corporation May Be the More Appropriate Structure

Scenario 1: Raising Venture Capital or Institutional Investment

A corporation can be particularly useful for businesses seeking institutional venture capital. Investors often expect a stock-based structure with clearly defined shares, preferred-stock rights, capitalization tables, and standardized equity documentation. Many venture-backed startups use a C corporation, frequently a Delaware corporation, because of investor familiarity and Delaware's established corporate legal system.

Scenario 2: Complex Equity Compensation

Corporations can issue stock and establish equity compensation arrangements such as incentive stock options when the applicable requirements are satisfied. LLCs generally use membership interests or other contractual equity arrangements rather than corporate stock.

Scenario 3: A Potential QSBS Strategy

Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) under Section 1202 is a potential federal tax benefit for qualifying stock in qualifying C corporations. The rules changed for qualifying stock acquired after July 4, 2025: 3-year holding period = 50% exclusion; 4-year = 75%; 5+ years = 100%. The applicable gain exclusion limit increased to $15 million. An LLC membership interest is not itself QSBS.

The Middle Path: LLC Taxed as a Corporation

An eligible LLC can generally elect to be classified as a corporation for federal tax purposes using Form 8832. An eligible LLC can also elect S-corporation taxation using Form 2553. An S-corp election can change the way owner compensation and business profits are treated for employment-tax purposes, but potential tax benefit should be compared with payroll costs, accounting costs, additional tax filings, and state tax treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

01 What is the main tax difference between an LLC and a corporation?

It depends on the LLC's federal tax classification. A single-member LLC generally defaults to disregarded-entity treatment, while a multi-member LLC generally defaults to partnership treatment. An LLC can also elect corporate tax treatment or, if eligible, S-corporation taxation. A C corporation generally pays federal income tax at the corporate level, and shareholders may owe additional tax when taxable dividends are distributed.

02 Can an LLC avoid double taxation?

A default LLC generally uses pass-through taxation rather than C-corporation taxation. This means the business's taxable income generally passes through to its owner or owners rather than being taxed first as C-corporation income. However, the exact treatment depends on the LLC's federal tax classification.

03 Is a C corporation ever more appropriate than an LLC for taxes?

It can be, depending on the business. A C corporation may be considered when a business expects to retain significant profits, has particular financing requirements, or is evaluating potential QSBS treatment. But a 21% corporate rate should not simply be compared with a personal marginal tax rate to determine which structure is cheaper.

04 Does a corporation offer better liability protection than an LLC?

Both LLCs and corporations can provide a liability shield between the entity and its owners. It is not accurate to say that one structure universally provides better protection. Personal liability can still arise from personal guarantees, personal misconduct, certain taxes, and other circumstances.

05 What are the annual compliance requirements for a corporation vs. an LLC?

Both structures can have state reporting and other compliance obligations. Corporations generally have a more formal governance structure involving shareholders, directors, officers, bylaws, and corporate records. LLCs generally have more flexibility, although they still need to follow applicable state requirements and maintain appropriate records.

06 Can I convert an LLC to a corporation later?

Often, yes. Depending on the state and circumstances, the process may involve a statutory conversion, forming a corporation and transferring assets, or another restructuring method. Tax consequences, contracts, licenses, ownership records, and financing arrangements should be reviewed before making the change.

07 Is an LLC or corporation better for a small business?

Neither is automatically better for every small business. An LLC may be a practical choice when the business is closely held and values flexible ownership and management. A corporation may be more appropriate when stock issuance, institutional investment, complex equity compensation, or potential QSBS treatment is important.

08 What is the difference between Articles of Organization and Articles of Incorporation?

Articles of Organization or a similarly named filing generally creates an LLC. Articles of Incorporation generally creates a corporation. The exact document name and required information vary by state.

09 Do I need a lawyer to form a corporation or an LLC?

Not necessarily. Many states allow owners to form LLCs and corporations directly through the state's filing system. Professional legal or tax advice can nevertheless be useful when the business involves multiple owners, outside investors, complex contracts, intellectual property, significant assets, stock options, or a planned tax election.
