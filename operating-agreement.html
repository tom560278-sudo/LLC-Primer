An LLC operating agreement sets the internal rules of a limited liability company: who owns it, who can act for it, how decisions are approved, how money moves, what happens when ownership changes, and how the company can be wound down. It is important, but it should not be sold as a magic document that automatically creates liability protection, guarantees a bank account, or makes a tax election by itself.

QUICK ANSWER:

A written operating agreement is usually the safest practical format even when state law permits an oral or implied agreement. The exact legal requirement, signature mechanics and default rules depend on the LLC's formation state.

What an LLC operating agreement actually does

The operating agreement is the LLC's private governance contract. It can define ownership percentages, capital contributions, voting thresholds, management authority, distributions, transfers, buyouts, admission of new members, succession planning and dissolution procedures.

What it can do
Replace many state default rules with agreed internal rules, allocate authority, document ownership and establish procedures for future changes.
≠
What it cannot do by itself
Create the LLC, override non-waivable law, guarantee limited liability, open a bank account automatically or elect federal tax status without the required IRS filing.

If you are still deciding how the entity itself works, start with our LLC structure guide. The operating agreement governs the company after formation; it does not replace the state formation filing.

Five states with especially explicit operating-agreement rules

California, Delaware, Maine, Missouri and New York all have statutes or official guidance that make the operating agreement especially important. The old version of this page treated them as if they imposed the same written-document rule. They do not.

Practical rule:

Even where an oral or implied agreement can exist, a written version is usually much easier to administer, prove, amend and show to a bank, lender, buyer or investor.

Single-member and multi-member agreements solve different problems

A single-member LLC does not need voting mechanics between owners, but a written agreement can still document ownership, management authority, succession instructions and the separation between the owner and the entity. It can also provide a clean record when a bank or counterparty asks who is authorized to act.

A multi-member LLC needs more detail because economic rights and control can diverge. The agreement should anticipate contributions, voting, deadlocks, transfers, departures, death or incapacity, buyouts and the process for admitting new members.

⚖️ Single-Member vs Multi-Member Agreement Comparison
TOPIC SINGLE-MEMBER MULTI-MEMBER
Ownership record Document sole ownership Document each member's interest
Voting Usually simple Define thresholds and reserved matters
Exit/buyout rules Succession is the main issue Buy-sell and transfer rules are central
Economic provisions Usually straightforward Must coordinate allocations, distributions and capital accounts
Dispute process Less central Often worth defining in advance

Trigger events, authorization and distribution mechanics consistent with applicable law.

Tax clauses need more care than the old page suggested

An operating agreement can describe the members' intended economic arrangement and can record the LLC's current tax posture. It does not itself elect federal tax classification.

📊 Tax Provisions Alignment Matrix
FEDERAL TAX TREATMENT WHAT THE AGREEMENT SHOULD RECOGNIZE
Single-member LLC — default disregarded entity There is only one owner, so partnership-style special allocations do not apply.
Multi-member LLC taxed as partnership Tax allocations under the partnership agreement are subject to IRC §704(b) and related regulations; cash distributions and tax allocations are not the same concept.
LLC taxed as C corporation The federal classification is made through the applicable IRS election, such as Form 8832 when required.
LLC taxed as S corporation The S election is made with Form 2553. Governing provisions should not create non-identical distribution or liquidation rights that could create a second class of stock problem.
Do not copy a "special allocation" clause casually.

Partnership allocations can be respected only under the federal partnership allocation rules, and an LLC taxed as an S corporation has a different one-class-of-stock constraint. Coordinate unusual economics with a tax professional before signing them into the agreement.

For the broader classification framework, see our LLC tax guide.

Management structure and authority should match the real business

The agreement should say whether the LLC is member-managed or manager-managed and then define what each person can actually do. Do not rely on a generic sentence saying a manager can "bind the company in all matters" if the members intend to reserve major decisions for themselves.

Member-managed
Useful when owners participate directly. Define ordinary authority, voting weight and which actions need approval beyond day-to-day management.
VS
Manager-managed
Useful when owners are passive or management is delegated. Define manager powers, removal, compensation and reserved member decisions.

Does every member have to sign?

There is no safe nationwide rule that says an operating agreement is unenforceable unless every member signs the same paper document. Delaware is the clearest counterexample: its LLC Act expressly says a member, manager or assignee can be bound by the LLC agreement whether or not that person executes it.

For a written agreement, collecting signatures or another clear form of assent from the people whose rights are being governed is still a strong documentation practice. But the legal effect of a missing signature depends on the formation state's law, the agreement itself and the facts showing assent.

Notarization:

Do not treat notarization as a universal validity requirement. It may be requested by a particular bank, lender, transaction or foreign-use process, but that is different from saying every LLC operating agreement must be notarized.

An operating agreement supports governance — it does not guarantee the liability shield

The absence of an operating agreement does not produce one automatic nationwide liability consequence. Limited-liability disputes are governed by state law and the full factual record.

A written agreement can be useful evidence that the owner intended to operate through a separate legal entity and can document governance, authority and recordkeeping. But veil-piercing and personal-liability questions are state-specific and usually depend on a broader set of facts, such as commingling, undercapitalization, personal guarantees, fraud, misuse of the entity and compliance with applicable law.

BETTER FRAMING
Use the operating agreement as one part of disciplined entity governance. Do not tell an owner that the document alone creates the shield or that its absence automatically destroys it.

Banks, lenders and investors may ask for it — but requirements vary

Financial institutions often need evidence of ownership and signing authority, and an operating agreement can be one way to provide it. However, no universal banking rule requires every LLC to present the document in every account-opening process.

A bank may instead or additionally request formation documents, EIN confirmation, beneficial-owner information, resolutions, identification or institution-specific forms. The operating agreement therefore helps as a governance and ownership record without guaranteeing approval.

For account-opening documentation, use our business bank account guide.

How to amend an operating agreement

Start with the amendment clause in the current agreement. It should tell you who can propose a change and what approval threshold applies. If the agreement is silent, the formation state's default LLC law can control the process.

Do not assume every amendment requires unanimous signatures. Some agreements require a majority, supermajority or consent of only the affected class; certain changes may require broader consent under non-waivable law. Record the approval, date the amendment and keep the current agreement together with its amendment history.

Five operating-agreement mistakes worth fixing before signing

⚠️ Drafting risks
1
Using the wrong state's template
The governing law and default rules must match the LLC's actual formation state.
2
Turning a tax clause into a fake election
Federal classification changes require the applicable IRS election; the agreement alone is not enough.
3
Mixing ownership, allocations and cash distributions
These concepts can differ, but the tax consequences and state-law economics have to work together.
4
Ignoring transfer and exit mechanics
Multi-member LLCs should know what happens when someone wants out, dies, becomes disabled or tries to transfer an interest.
5
Leaving the agreement frozen after the company changes
Ownership, management authority, major financing and tax-classification changes can make old provisions inaccurate.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS

Operating Agreement Questions

01 Can a sole owner create a valid operating agreement?

Yes. Single-member LLC operating agreements are valid and routinely used to establish sole ownership, management authority, banking resolution terms, and succession rules.

02 Do I have to file my Operating Agreement with the Secretary of State?

No. Operating agreements are internal private contracts and are not filed with the state Secretary of State office in any U.S. state. Keep it stored with your official company records.

03 Does an Operating Agreement need to be notarized?

Notarization is generally not required by state law for validity, although some banks or lenders may request a notarized signature during account opening or financing.
