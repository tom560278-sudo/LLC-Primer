How to Form a US LLC as a Non-US Resident (2026 Guide)

A non-U.S. resident can generally form and own a U.S. LLC without being a U.S. citizen or U.S. resident. U.S. states generally allow foreign individuals to own LLC interests, although the exact formation requirements, registered-agent rules, tax obligations, and business licensing requirements depend on the state and the owner's circumstances.

You generally do not need a U.S. Social Security Number simply to form an LLC, although an SSN or other taxpayer identification information may be relevant for certain federal tax or banking matters.

Whether you're considering a Wyoming LLC as a foreign owner, applying for an EIN without an SSN, opening a U.S. business bank account remotely, or understanding the tax obligations of a foreign-owned LLC, this guide explains the main steps and considerations.

You'll learn how to form an LLC in the USA as a non-resident, how to compare states, what the formation and recurring costs can include, which federal filings may apply, and what to consider before opening a U.S. business account.

Quick answer: Yes. A non-U.S. resident can generally own a U.S. LLC without U.S. citizenship, permanent residency, or a U.S. Social Security Number. You generally need to choose a state, file the required formation document, maintain an eligible registered agent, and complete the federal tax-ID process applicable to your business.

However, forming the LLC is only the first step. A foreign-owned LLC may have U.S. federal tax filing obligations even when the business does not owe U.S. federal income tax, and banking or payment-platform approval is a separate decision made by the financial institution.

1. Can a non-US resident form a US LLC?

Yes. A non-U.S. resident can generally own a U.S. LLC without becoming a U.S. citizen or obtaining a U.S. visa.

A non-resident can generally own a single-member LLC or hold an interest in a multi-member LLC alongside other foreign or U.S. owners. There is generally no requirement that an LLC have a U.S.-citizen owner.

The formation process itself is primarily a state-law process. You choose a state, select an available business name, appoint a registered agent with an appropriate in-state address, and file the state's formation document.

However, forming an LLC does not automatically give the owner a right to work physically in the United States or establish U.S. immigration status. If you plan to perform services while physically present in the United States, immigration and tax rules need to be considered separately.

ℹ️ Single-member vs. multi-member: A non-U.S. resident can generally use either structure. The federal tax treatment and reporting obligations can differ depending on the number of owners, elections made, and the LLC's activities.

2. What you need — and what you don't

The practical requirements depend on the state and your business circumstances, but the basic formation process is relatively straightforward.

What you generally need

  • Registered agent — The LLC generally must maintain an eligible registered agent in its formation state. A commercial registered-agent provider can be used when the owner does not have an appropriate person or address in the state.
  • Articles of Organization or equivalent formation document — This is the state filing that creates the LLC. The document, filing fee, and processing procedure depend on the state.
  • EIN, when applicable — An Employer Identification Number is the LLC's federal tax identification number. Many LLCs need one for federal tax, banking, employees, or other business purposes. The IRS provides the EIN application process.
  • Owner identification information — Federal and financial institutions may require identity and taxpayer information for the owner or responsible party. A passport may be requested by a bank or service provider, but the exact documentation is provider-specific.

For an EIN application, the IRS's current Form SS-4 instructions state that a responsible party who does not have and is ineligible to obtain an SSN or ITIN can enter “foreign” or “N/A” on the responsible-party identification field.

What you generally don't need simply to form the LLC

  • U.S. citizenship
  • U.S. permanent residency
  • A U.S. visa solely for ownership of the LLC
  • A U.S. Social Security Number solely for state LLC formation
  • A U.S. bank account before the LLC is formed
  • A physical visit to the United States solely to create the LLC

However, these points should not be interpreted as a guarantee that every bank, payment processor, marketplace, or financial service will accept a non-U.S. owner without additional documentation.

💡 A virtual business address may be useful for business correspondence or customer-facing purposes. It is different from a registered-agent address, and whether a particular address can be used for banking, licensing, state filings, or other purposes depends on the provider and the applicable rules.

3. Choosing the right state

There is no single U.S. state that is automatically appropriate for every non-resident founder. The right choice depends on factors such as where the business operates, where the owner conducts business, state fees, tax treatment, registered-agent costs, privacy considerations, and future financing plans.

For a founder operating entirely outside the United States with no U.S. operating location, states such as Wyoming, Delaware, and New Mexico are often considered because of their established LLC systems and different fee structures. But the lowest filing fee does not necessarily mean the lowest overall cost, and forming in a different state can create additional registration obligations if the business actually operates elsewhere.

Important 2026 BOI update

U.S.-formed LLCs are currently exempt from FinCEN beneficial ownership information (BOI) reporting. FinCEN's August 2026 final rule states that companies created in the United States are exempt from BOI reporting requirements. This exemption applies to the U.S.-formed entity even when its owner is a non-U.S. person.

This is important because older guides may still tell foreign-owned U.S. LLCs that they must file a BOI report simply because the owner is foreign. That information is outdated under the current 2026 rule.

The BOI rules are different for certain foreign entities formed under foreign law that register to do business in the United States, so do not confuse a U.S.-formed LLC with a foreign company registering to operate in the U.S.

Important federal tax filing consideration for foreign-owned LLCs

A foreign-owned single-member LLC that is treated as a disregarded entity for U.S. federal income-tax purposes can still have an information-reporting obligation.

The IRS states that a foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity generally must file a pro forma Form 1120 with Form 5472 attached when the Form 5472 rules apply. This can apply even though the disregarded entity itself generally does not file a regular income-tax return.

This is one of the most important distinctions for non-U.S. founders: “I do not owe U.S. income tax” does not necessarily mean “I have no U.S. tax filing obligations.”

Because the result depends on ownership, transactions, tax classification, U.S. activities, and the owner's circumstances, a qualified U.S. tax professional should be consulted before assuming that no federal filing is required.

4. How to form your US LLC as a non-resident

The formation process can often be completed remotely from your home country. The formation process itself can be relatively quick, but obtaining an EIN and completing banking or compliance checks can take additional time.

1
Choose your state
Wyoming, Delaware, and New Mexico are commonly considered by non-resident founders for different reasons. Wyoming may appeal to businesses looking for relatively low state-level recurring costs; Delaware is commonly used by companies that expect institutional investment; and New Mexico does not require domestic LLCs to file an annual report. You don't necessarily need to operate in the state where you form the LLC. However, if your business actually operates in another U.S. state, you may need to register there as a foreign LLC and comply with that state's taxes, fees, and other requirements.
2
Choose your registered agent
Every LLC generally needs a registered agent with an eligible physical address in the formation state. As a non-resident, you'll commonly use a commercial registered-agent service. Northwest Registered Agent offers registered-agent services in Wyoming and other states. Check its current offer and renewal terms before purchasing, as promotional pricing and renewal rates can change.
3
File Articles of Organization
This document officially creates your LLC under state law. Submit it to the appropriate state filing office with the applicable state filing fee. Wyoming provides online filing, but processing times can change depending on filing volume and whether expedited processing is selected. Check the Wyoming Secretary of State for the current processing information before filing.
4
Draft an operating agreement
Not always required by state law, but an operating agreement can establish ownership percentages, member rights, management responsibilities, voting procedures, and how profits and distributions are handled. Banks, payment processors, investors, and other counterparties may request organizational documents, although requirements differ between providers. An operating agreement can therefore be useful to have before applying for business financial accounts.
5
Apply for your EIN
Your EIN is your LLC's federal tax identification number. If your principal place of business is outside the United States, the IRS does not make the standard online EIN application available. International applicants can apply by calling the IRS international line at +1 (267) 941-1099, or by submitting Form SS-4 by fax or mail. The IRS states that international applicants can generally receive an EIN by fax within approximately 4 business days. Mail applications can take approximately 4–5 weeks. Telephone processing is available to eligible applicants, but issuance should not be described as guaranteed same-day service. Processing times can change, so check the current IRS Form SS-4 instructions before applying.
6
Set up your annual compliance calendar
Note your LLC's state filing and tax deadlines as soon as the company is formed. Wyoming LLCs generally have an annual report due on the first day of the month in which the anniversary of formation occurs. Also record your registered-agent renewal date and any federal tax or information-reporting deadlines that apply to your LLC. A foreign-owned LLC may have Form 5472 reporting obligations when the applicable reportable-transaction requirements are met; this should not be treated as an automatic filing requirement for every non-resident LLC. Keeping these deadlines in one calendar can help prevent missed filings, late fees, or loss of good standing.

5. Getting your EIN without an SSN

If your principal place of business is outside the United States, you generally cannot use the IRS online EIN application. International applicants can apply by phone, fax, or mail.

Call the IRS international line at +1 (267) 941-1099 with a completed Form SS-4 available. The IRS provides telephone assistance to international applicants, but same-day issuance should not be treated as guaranteed.

You can also fax Form SS-4 to 304-707-9471 from outside the United States. The IRS states that international applicants can generally receive an EIN by fax within approximately 4 business days. Mail applications can take approximately 4–5 weeks. Processing times can change during periods of higher IRS volume.

Once your EIN is issued, keep the IRS confirmation documentation with your company records. Banks and financial platforms may request EIN documentation, but their requirements differ. The IRS may provide a replacement EIN verification letter, known as Letter 147C, when appropriate.

⚠️ Don't use another person's SSN or an inaccurate nominee arrangement to obtain an EIN. Complete Form SS-4 using the responsible-party information and identification instructions that apply to your circumstances.

7. Taxes for foreign-owned LLCs

This is one of the most important parts of forming a U.S. LLC as a non-resident. U.S. tax treatment depends on the LLC's federal tax classification, the type and source of its income, where business activities take place, and the owner's individual circumstances.

A U.S. LLC does not automatically create the same tax result for every non-resident owner. Formation, federal income tax, information reporting, and tax obligations in the owner's home country are separate issues.

Pass-through taxation

A U.S. LLC is generally treated as a pass-through structure for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects corporate taxation. A single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity, while a multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership unless an election is made to change its classification.

For a non-resident owner, the U.S. tax result depends on the type and source of income and whether the owner is engaged in a U.S. trade or business.

U.S.-source income that is effectively connected with a U.S. trade or business is generally reported and taxed under the rules applicable to non-resident aliens. A non-resident alien who is required to file a U.S. income-tax return generally uses Form 1040-NR. Foreign-source income is generally outside U.S. taxation for a non-resident alien unless specific rules cause it to be treated as effectively connected income.

See our LLC taxes guide for a broader explanation of LLC federal tax classifications.

Effectively Connected Income (ECI)

Effectively Connected Income is generally income connected with the conduct of a U.S. trade or business.

If a foreign person is engaged in a U.S. trade or business, U.S.-source income connected with that business is generally treated as ECI and, after allowable deductions, taxed at graduated rates. Whether a person is engaged in a U.S. trade or business depends on the actual activities involved.

The analysis is more complicated than simply asking whether the company has a U.S. bank account, employees, office, or customers. For example, where services are physically performed, how the business operates, and whether other U.S. activities are involved can affect the result.

Likewise, operating a business from outside the United States does not by itself guarantee that no U.S. tax or filing obligation exists. Specific income-source and entity-classification rules can still apply.

Non-resident owners with meaningful U.S. business activity should obtain professional advice before determining whether their income is ECI.

Form 5472 — an important filing for some foreign-owned LLCs

If you own a single-member U.S. LLC as a non-U.S. person and the LLC is treated as a foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity, Form 5472 may be required when the applicable reportable-transaction rules are met.

A foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity that is required to file Form 5472 generally attaches the form to a pro forma Form 1120. The IRS specifically states that the disregarded entity does not otherwise have a regular corporate income-tax return requirement, but must use the pro forma Form 1120 for this information-reporting purpose.

Form 5472 should therefore not be described as an automatic filing for every foreign-owned LLC. The requirement depends on the entity's classification and whether reportable transactions occurred.

For a foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity that is required to file, the filing is generally due by the due date of the applicable Form 1120, including extensions. For a calendar-year entity, the normal corporate return deadline is generally the 15th day of the fourth month after the end of the tax year.

The penalty for failing to file Form 5472 when required is $25,000, with additional penalties possible when a failure continues after IRS notification.

🚨 Do not assume that every foreign-owned LLC must file Form 5472 — but do not assume that having little or no U.S. income means there are no federal filing obligations. Foreign-owned LLCs should have their reporting requirements reviewed by a tax professional familiar with international U.S. entities.

Multi-member LLCs with foreign members

A multi-member LLC is generally taxed as a partnership for federal income-tax purposes unless it elects a different classification.

A partnership with foreign partners can have additional U.S. withholding and reporting obligations. If the partnership has effectively connected taxable income allocable to foreign partners, Section 1446 withholding may apply. For 2026, the IRS states that the withholding rate is generally 37% for non-corporate foreign partners and 21% for corporate foreign partners. Different withholding rules can apply to other types of U.S.-source payments, and treaty provisions may affect certain results.

The commonly referenced 30% rate applies to certain U.S.-source FDAP income that is not effectively connected with a U.S. trade or business, subject to applicable treaty reductions. It should not be presented as the universal withholding rate for foreign LLC members.

Tax treaties

The United States has income-tax treaties with numerous countries. Treaty provisions can affect withholding rates and the taxation of particular types of income.

Whether treaty benefits apply depends on factors such as the owner's country of residence, the type and source of income, the owner's tax status, and the applicable treaty provisions.

A tax treaty does not automatically eliminate U.S. filing or information-reporting requirements. Cross-border businesses should obtain country-specific tax advice when treaty treatment is relevant.

8. Ongoing compliance requirements

Forming the LLC is a one-time state-law event. Maintaining the company can involve recurring state filings or taxes, registered-agent requirements, and federal tax or information-reporting obligations.

BOI report (FinCEN) — 2026 update

As of August 11, 2026, FinCEN's current rule exempts U.S.-formed companies from federal BOI reporting requirements. FinCEN states that U.S. companies are no longer required to file BOI reports under the current rule.

This means a domestic U.S. LLC formed in Wyoming, Delaware, New Mexico, or another U.S. state is currently exempt from BOI reporting even when its owner is a non-U.S. person.

The rules are different for certain entities formed under foreign law and registered to do business in the United States. Those entities may fall within the current definition of a reporting company and may have BOI obligations unless an exemption applies.

Because BOI rules have changed substantially since 2024, use the current FinCEN guidance when checking an entity's status rather than relying on older formation guides.

Annual report

State recurring requirements depend on where the LLC is formed and where it does business.

For example:

  • Wyoming: The annual report is generally due on the first day of the LLC's anniversary month. The minimum annual license tax is $60, subject to the state's asset-based calculation.
  • Delaware: Delaware LLCs generally do not file an annual report but must pay a $300 annual LLC tax, generally due June 1.
  • New Mexico: Domestic LLCs generally do not file an annual report, although other state tax, registration, licensing, or business requirements may still apply.

A lack of an annual report requirement does not necessarily mean that a state has no recurring obligations.

Registered agent renewal

Your registered agent must remain eligible and active under the law of the state where the LLC is registered.

If the registered-agent appointment lapses, the LLC can have difficulty receiving service of process and official state correspondence and may face state-level compliance consequences.

Registered-agent service also does not automatically include annual reports, tax filings, licenses, or federal information returns unless those services are separately provided.

Form 5472 (when applicable)

A foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity that has the applicable reportable transactions may be required to file Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120. This is a federal information-reporting requirement and is separate from any individual income-tax return that may apply to the owner.

For a calendar-year entity, the normal deadline is generally April 15, subject to applicable extensions. The IRS permits a qualifying foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity to request an extension using Form 7004.

The important point is that Form 5472 should be evaluated based on the LLC's actual classification and transactions rather than treated as automatically required for every foreign-owned LLC.

9. How much does a US LLC cost for a non-resident?

The cost of forming and maintaining a U.S. LLC depends on the state, registered-agent provider, tax and bookkeeping needs, and whether you use paid formation or compliance services.

Wyoming example

Year 1: Wyoming charges $100 to file Articles of Organization. A commercial registered-agent service is an additional cost. If using a provider offering a $39 first-year or promotional registered-agent rate, the total would be approximately $139 plus any applicable service or processing fees.

An EIN is free when obtained directly from the IRS. Using a third-party EIN service is optional and adds a service fee.

Year 2+: Wyoming's minimum annual license tax is $60. Add the registered-agent renewal price charged by your provider and any applicable service fees.

Do not assume that a provider's first-year promotional price is the same as its renewal price.

Delaware example

Delaware's current LLC formation fee should be checked against the state's official fee schedule before filing. Delaware LLCs generally pay a $300 annual tax, due June 1, and do not file an annual report. Late payment carries a $200 penalty plus interest.

The total cost therefore includes:

  • Delaware formation fee
  • $300 annual LLC tax
  • Registered-agent service
  • Any optional formation or compliance services
  • Professional accounting or tax costs, if needed

Delaware may be relevant for businesses considering institutional investment or other Delaware-specific legal and governance considerations, but the additional cost should be weighed against the actual needs of the business rather than assuming that every non-resident founder needs a Delaware LLC.

Other costs to consider

Non-resident founders may also encounter:

  • Form 5472/pro forma Form 1120 preparation: professional fees depend on the CPA or tax service and the complexity of the LLC's transactions.
  • Bookkeeping: depends on transaction volume and the service selected.
  • Registered-agent renewal: renewal pricing can differ from the initial promotional rate.
  • Foreign qualification: additional state filing fees and recurring obligations may apply if the LLC actually conducts business in another state.
  • State and local taxes: depend on where the business operates and what it sells or does.
  • Banking and payment processing: provider-specific fees and eligibility requirements apply.
  • Licenses and permits: depend on the business activity and location.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a non-US resident form a US LLC?
Yes. A non-US resident can generally form and own a US LLC without being a US citizen or resident and without having a Social Security Number (SSN). The exact formation requirements are set by the state where you form the LLC. Common requirements include choosing a business name, appointing an eligible registered agent with an in-state address, and filing Articles of Organization or the state's equivalent formation document.

An EIN is often needed for banking, tax, and other business purposes, but it is obtained separately from the state LLC formation process. Additional requirements can apply depending on the business, state, ownership structure, and activities.
Do I need an SSN to form a US LLC?
No. An SSN is generally not required to form a US LLC. It is also possible for an international applicant without a US principal place of business to obtain an EIN without an SSN or ITIN. The IRS says international applicants who do not have a US legal residence, principal place of business, or principal office or agency cannot use the online EIN application and may apply by phone, fax, or mail instead.

An SSN may be required for other US tax or identification purposes depending on your personal circumstances, but that is separate from the basic LLC formation process.
Do I need an ITIN to form a US LLC?
No. An ITIN is not generally required to form an LLC or obtain an EIN. The IRS specifically allows certain foreign applicants completing Form SS-4 to enter "N/A" for the SSN or ITIN field when they are not eligible for or do not have one.

An ITIN may become relevant if you have a US federal individual tax filing requirement and are not eligible for an SSN. For example, a nonresident alien engaged in a US trade or business may need to file Form 1040-NR.
Can I get an EIN without an SSN?
Yes. If your principal place of business is outside the United States, the IRS says you cannot use its online EIN application. International applicants can apply by calling +1 267-941-1099, Monday through Friday, 6 a.m. to 11 p.m. Eastern Time, or by submitting Form SS-4 by fax or mail. The IRS currently states that international fax applications can generally be processed within about 4 business days, while mailed applications should be submitted at least 4–5 weeks before the EIN is needed.

The IRS does not guarantee that every phone application will result in a same-day EIN, so avoid treating same-day issuance as a guaranteed processing time. An authorized third party may also assist with the application.
What is the best state for a non-US resident to form an LLC?
There is no single state that is appropriate for every non-US resident. The relevant factors can include where the business actually operates, state formation and recurring fees, tax treatment, privacy considerations, banking or investor requirements, and whether the business will need to register in another state.

Wyoming is often considered by remote businesses because its LLC formation fee is $100 and its minimum annual license tax is $60. Delaware charges LLCs a $300 annual tax and generally does not require LLCs to file an annual report.

If the business will actually operate in another state, forming in Wyoming or Delaware does not necessarily eliminate the need to register there as a foreign LLC or comply with that state's taxes and requirements.
Can a non-resident open a US business bank account?
Yes, but approval depends on the financial institution or platform and its eligibility, identity-verification, compliance, and business requirements. A US LLC does not automatically qualify for a US business bank account.

Applicants may be asked for documents such as the LLC's formation documents, EIN, ownership information, identification, business details, and proof of address. Requirements can also differ based on the owner's country of residence and the nature of the business.

Mercury, for example, has specific eligibility requirements for companies with founders outside the United States, while other financial platforms use different requirements. Approval is not guaranteed.

Wise Business is a financial-services platform rather than a traditional US bank, so its account features and protections should not be treated as identical to those of a bank.
What is Form 5472 and do I need to file it?
Form 5472 is an IRS information return that can apply to a foreign-owned US disregarded entity, including certain foreign-owned single-member LLCs, when the entity has reportable transactions with related parties or certain other reportable transactions.

It is not correct to say that every foreign-owned single-member LLC automatically has to file Form 5472. Whether the filing is required depends on the entity's classification and transactions during the tax year. When required, a foreign-owned US disregarded entity generally attaches Form 5472 to a pro forma Form 1120.

The IRS penalty for failing to file a required Form 5472, or for filing an incomplete form, is $25,000, with additional continuation penalties possible after IRS notification.

Because the filing rules are technical, foreign-owned LLCs should confirm their specific reporting obligations with a qualified US tax professional.
Do non-resident LLC owners pay US taxes?
It depends on the LLC's tax classification, the owner's circumstances, the type and source of income, and whether the business is engaged in a US trade or business.

For nonresident individuals, income that is effectively connected with a US trade or business is generally subject to US federal income tax under the applicable rules. Certain US-source income that is not effectively connected may instead be subject to withholding, often at 30% or a lower treaty rate where applicable.

Simply owning a US LLC does not by itself determine the owner's final US tax liability. Foreign-source income, services performed outside the United States, tax treaties, LLC classification, and state-level obligations can all affect the analysis.

Form 5472 is an information-reporting requirement and should not be confused with an income-tax charge.
Do I need to file a BOI report for my US LLC?
For a US LLC formed in a US state, generally no under the current FinCEN rules. FinCEN's final rule, effective August 14, 2026, exempts companies created in the United States from BOI reporting requirements.

The current rules primarily concern certain entities formed under foreign law that register to do business in the United States. Those entities can have separate BOI reporting obligations and deadlines.

Because BOI requirements have changed substantially, use the current FinCEN guidance rather than older articles or formation checklists when determining whether a filing is required.
Can a foreigner own 100% of a US LLC?
Yes. A non-US person can generally own 100% of a US LLC, including as its sole member. Multiple foreign owners can also hold membership interests in the same LLC.

However, ownership rules can differ for particular regulated industries or transactions. Federal, state, and industry-specific restrictions may apply in areas such as certain aviation, communications, defense-related, or other regulated activities. The LLC structure itself does not eliminate those separate rules.
Is Wyoming really the best state for non-US residents?
Wyoming can be a practical option for some non-US-owned businesses, but it should not be treated as a universal default. Wyoming currently charges a $100 LLC formation fee and has a minimum annual license tax of $60, while its annual report is tied to the LLC's anniversary month.

The appropriate state can depend on where the business actually operates, whether it expects outside investment, recurring state costs, tax considerations, privacy concerns, and whether another state will require foreign qualification.

For a business physically operating in another state, forming in Wyoming does not automatically avoid that state's registration or tax requirements.
How long does it take to form a US LLC as a non-resident?
The state formation process and the EIN process are separate. Processing time for an LLC depends on the state, filing method, and any expedited service selected. Wyoming, for example, provides online filing and offers expedited review options for eligible filings, but processing times can change.

For the EIN, the IRS currently allows international applicants to apply by phone, fax, or mail when their principal place of business is outside the United States. The IRS states that international fax applications can generally be processed within about 4 business days, while mailed applications take approximately 4 weeks.

Bank-account approval is a separate process and should not be assumed to occur immediately after receiving the EIN. Financial institutions may conduct their own identity, business, address, and compliance reviews.
