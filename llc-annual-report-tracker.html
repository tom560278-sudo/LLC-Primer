LLC Annual Report Deadline Tracker — All 50 States (2026)

7 months ago · Updated 2 weeks ago

Every LLC in the United States has a compliance deadline most owners don't know about until it's too late — the LLC annual report. Miss it, and your state will hit you with a late fee. Ignore it long enough, and your LLC gets administratively dissolved. That means another business can legally register your company name, your liability protection disappears, and you have to pay reinstatement fees on top of everything else. This free LLC annual report deadline tracker exists specifically to prevent that from happening to you.

Why annual report rules vary so much from state to state

Every state handles annual reports differently. Some states call it a Statement of Information. Others call it an Annual Registration, a Periodic Report, or a Franchise Tax Return. A few states charge nothing at all — while others charge up to $500. Deadlines range from fixed calendar dates like April 15 to rolling anniversary-based dates tied to the month your LLC was formed. Meanwhile, four states require no report at all. Understanding exactly what your state requires is therefore not optional — it is a fundamental part of running a compliant LLC.

What Is an LLC Annual Report?

An LLC annual report is a mandatory filing that most states require every LLC to submit on a regular schedule — typically once a year, though some states require it every two years or even every ten. Despite the name, it is not a financial report and has nothing to do with your income, expenses, or taxes. Instead, it serves one specific purpose: confirming to the state that your LLC still exists, is still operating, and that your registered contact information is current and accurate.

What information does an annual report actually contain?

The required information varies by state, but most LLC annual reports ask for the same core details. Specifically, you typically need to confirm or update your LLC's legal name, principal business address, the name and address of your registered agent, and the names of your members or managers. In some states — particularly those with anniversary-based deadlines — the form is pre-filled with your current information, and you simply verify it and pay the fee. In others, however, you must complete the form from scratch each year.

Why states require it — and why it matters to you

States use the annual report system for two main reasons. First, it allows the state to maintain an accurate, current database of every active business entity — which is the same database you search when you use our Secretary of State Business Search tool. Second, it generates revenue. Annual report fees are a significant source of income for most state governments. Consequently, states take non-compliance seriously — and the penalties for missing a deadline are real.

ℹ️ It goes by many names depending on your state: Depending on where your LLC is registered, you may see this filing called an Annual Report, Statement of Information, Annual Registration, Periodic Report, Annual Renewal, Annual Statement, Biennial Report, or Franchise Tax Return. Regardless of what your state calls it, the underlying purpose is the same — and the consequences of missing it are equally real no matter what name appears on the form.

What Happens If You Miss Your LLC Annual Report Deadline

Missing an LLC annual report deadline triggers a predictable and escalating sequence of consequences. Understanding this sequence is important — not to scare you, but because the earlier you catch a missed filing, the cheaper and easier it is to fix. Most states give you a grace period before the most serious consequences kick in.

The 4-stage consequence timeline

1
IMMEDIATELY AFTER THE DEADLINE PASSES — Late fee applied to your account
Most states automatically add a late filing penalty to your LLC's account the moment the deadline passes. These range from $10 (Michigan, Colorado) to $400 (Florida after September 1). At this stage, however, the fix is simple: file the report and pay the penalty. Your LLC remains in good standing.
2
30-90 DAYS AFTER THE DEADLINE — LLC status changes to "Delinquent" or "Not in Good Standing"
After a grace period — typically 30 to 90 days depending on the state — your LLC's status in the Secretary of State database changes to Delinquent, Past Due, or Not in Good Standing. Consequently, you can no longer open a business bank account, get a loan, enter into contracts that require good standing certificates, or register as a foreign LLC in other states until the status is resolved.
3
90-180 DAYS AFTER THE DEADLINE — Administrative dissolution — your LLC is officially inactive
Beyond the delinquency window, if the late filing goes unresolved for an extended period, the state administratively dissolves your LLC. This is the most serious consequence — and notably, it has cascading effects. Your liability protection disappears, meaning your personal assets are no longer shielded from business debts. Additionally, your LLC name becomes available for other businesses to register. Your operating agreement and any contracts your LLC has signed may also be affected.
4
AFTER DISSOLUTION — Reinstatement — more expensive and time-consuming than just filing on time
Most states allow you to reinstate a dissolved LLC by filing a reinstatement application, paying all back annual report fees, and paying a reinstatement penalty — which is typically $100-$300 on top of everything else. Some states, however, require you to re-form the LLC entirely if the dissolution has been in place for more than a year. As a result, the total cost of reinstatement often exceeds $500 — compared to a $10-$75 on-time filing fee.
🚨 Dissolved LLC = your name is up for grabs: Once your LLC is administratively dissolved, your registered business name immediately becomes available for other entities to register. If a competitor or anyone else registers it first, you cannot use it for your reinstated LLC — and you may have to rebrand entirely. This is one of the most overlooked and painful consequences of missing an annual report deadline.

States That Don't Require an LLC Annual Report

A small number of states have eliminated the annual report requirement for LLCs entirely. If you're forming a new LLC and want to minimize ongoing compliance obligations, these states are worth considering — though it's important to understand that "no annual report" does not mean "zero ongoing compliance." Other requirements may still apply.

Arizona and Missouri — minimal ongoing compliance

🌵 Arizona — No annual report required

Arizona LLCs have no annual reporting obligation. However, you must maintain a statutory agent (registered agent) in the state and publish a formation notice in a newspaper when you first form. After that, ongoing compliance is minimal — one of the main reasons Arizona is increasingly popular for LLC formation.

🎷 Missouri — No annual report required

Missouri LLCs have no annual report obligation. Combined with no franchise tax and a relatively low $50 formation fee, Missouri offers some of the lowest ongoing compliance costs in the country. Notably, you still need to maintain a registered agent in Missouri and keep your business information current with the state.

New Mexico and South Carolina — privacy and county-level nuances

🌶️ New Mexico — No annual report required

New Mexico is one of the most privacy-friendly states for LLC formation, and also requires no annual report. As a result, it's popular among online business owners who want minimal ongoing obligations. Additionally, New Mexico does not require member names to be disclosed in formation documents — adding another layer of privacy.

🌴 South Carolina — No annual report required for most LLCs

South Carolina does not require LLCs to file an annual report with the Secretary of State. However, LLCs in South Carolina may owe Business Personal Property Tax filed annually with the county — so while the SOS report is eliminated, a separate county-level filing may still apply depending on your business assets.

ℹ️ Pennsylvania — the outlier: one report every 10 years: Pennsylvania sits in a category of its own. Rather than an annual or biennial report, Pennsylvania requires only a Decennial Report — once every ten years — for just $7. For most practical purposes this is effectively no ongoing reporting burden, which makes Pennsylvania an underrated choice for low-maintenance LLC compliance.

How to File Your LLC Annual Report — Step by Step

Filing an LLC annual report is generally straightforward once you know exactly what your state requires. The process takes 5–15 minutes in most states and can be completed entirely online. Below is the standard workflow that applies across the majority of states.

Before you start — what to have ready

1
Confirm your state's deadline and current filing fee
Use the tracker above to check your exact due date and fee before you start. Fees occasionally change year to year, and filing in the wrong period (especially for biennial filers) is a common mistake. Specifically, biennial filers sometimes file in the wrong year and either miss their actual deadline or file a duplicate — both of which cause complications.
2
Gather your LLC's current information
Most states ask you to confirm or update your LLC's legal name, principal office address, registered agent name and address, and the names of members or managers. If any of this information has changed since your last filing — for example, you moved, changed your registered agent, or added a member — this is the moment to update it officially. Additionally, have your LLC's state-issued entity number or filing ID ready, as most online portals require it to locate your account.
3
Open the official state portal — not a third-party service
Use the direct link in the tracker above to open your state's official filing portal. Avoid third-party sites that charge a service fee to "help you file" — they are simply middlemen that charge $50–$200 to fill out a form you can complete yourself in 10 minutes for the standard state fee. Consequently, using the official portal directly always saves you money and gives you a direct confirmation of receipt from the state.

Filing and confirming your submission

4
Complete the form and pay the filing fee
Most state portals walk you through the form step by step. In many states — particularly those with pre-filled anniversary-based systems — you simply review your existing information, confirm it's accurate, and pay. The filing fee is typically paid by credit or debit card. Some states also accept ACH bank transfers. After payment, you will generally receive an immediate on-screen confirmation and a confirmation email.
5
Save your confirmation and update your LLC records
Download or screenshot the filing confirmation immediately. Save it alongside your LLC's formation documents — your Articles of Organization, operating agreement, and EIN confirmation letter. Some lenders, landlords, and contract partners ask for proof of good standing when you enter into agreements, and your annual report confirmation is part of that compliance record. Additionally, set your next year's reminder in your calendar before you close the portal tab.
6
Verify your LLC status in the state database after filing
Within 1–5 business days of filing, run a quick search on your LLC using our Secretary of State Business Search tool to confirm your status shows as "Active" or "Good Standing." In most states the update is immediate or same day, but in some states — notably Maryland and North Carolina — processing takes a few days. If your status doesn't update within a week, contact the SOS office directly to confirm receipt.

Annual Report Filed — What to Check and Do Next

Filing your annual report is not the end of your LLC's compliance obligations for the year. Instead, it's a useful prompt to run through a quick compliance checklist. Here are the most important follow-up steps — particularly relevant if anything has changed in your LLC over the past year.

Post-filing compliance checklist

✓ Verify your registered agent is still current and reachable

Your registered agent is the entity that receives official legal and government mail on behalf of your LLC. If you've moved, changed agents, or your agent's address has changed, update that information with the state immediately — not just on the annual report, but as a separate filing in most states. An unreachable registered agent is one of the most common causes of missed legal notices.

✓ Confirm your operating agreement reflects current ownership

If your LLC gained or lost members, changed ownership percentages, or modified its management structure during the year, update your operating agreement accordingly. The annual report is a useful annual trigger for this review — and an outdated operating agreement is a liability in any legal or financial dispute involving your LLC.

Tax obligations and setting your next reminder

✓ Check whether any state tax obligations have changed

Some states impose franchise taxes, gross receipts taxes, or business privilege taxes separately from the annual report. Texas, for example, requires a Franchise Tax Report annually regardless of whether your revenue exceeds the threshold. Additionally, if your LLC's revenue has grown, you may have crossed thresholds that trigger new tax or licensing requirements. Our LLC taxes guide covers the most common state-level tax obligations.

✓ Set your next deadline reminder — before you close this page

Add next year's filing deadline to your calendar right now, with a 30-day advance reminder. If your state uses an anniversary-based system, the due month is tied to your LLC's formation month — not a fixed calendar date. Frequently Asked Questions

What happens if I file my LLC annual report late?
If you file late, your state will impose a late fee ranging from $10 to $400 depending on the state. If it remains unpaid after 30-90 days, your LLC status changes to "Not in Good Standing." If ignored for 90-180 days, the state will administratively dissolve your LLC.
Is an LLC annual report the same as filing taxes?
No. An annual report is a state corporate compliance filing that verifies your business address and registered agent details. It has nothing to do with your income, revenue, or federal tax returns (IRS Form 1040 / 1065 / 1120S).
Which states do not require an LLC annual report?
Arizona, Missouri, New Mexico, and South Carolina require no annual report from LLCs with the Secretary of State. Pennsylvania requires a report only once every 10 years (Decennial Report).
Can I file my LLC annual report myself without paying a service fee?
Yes! You can file directly on your state's Secretary of State official website in 10-15 minutes and pay only the state filing fee. You do not need to pay third-party filing services.
What if my LLC had no income or business activity this year?
You must still file the annual report and pay the state fee. The annual report maintains your legal corporate existence with the state regardless of whether your business made money.
