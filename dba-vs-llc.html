DBA vs LLC (2026)

Most people searching DBA vs LLC are comparing two things that are not actually alternatives. A DBA is generally a registered business name. An LLC is a legal business entity. They can work together rather than compete with each other.

A DBA (Doing Business As) — also called a fictitious business name, assumed name, or trade name in some jurisdictions — allows a person or existing business entity to operate under a name different from its legal name. It does not, by itself, create a separate legal entity or provide limited liability protection.


An LLC (Limited Liability Company) is a legal business structure created under state law. An LLC can provide liability protection between the business and its owners, subject to applicable law and the circumstances of a particular claim.

What Is a DBA (Doing Business As)?

A DBA — short for "Doing Business As" — is generally a registration that allows a person or business to operate under a name different from its legal name. The terminology varies by jurisdiction: fictitious name, assumed name, trade name, or DBA. Registering a DBA does not itself provide legal protection.

What a DBA Actually Does

  • Allows operation under a business name different from your personal legal name
  • Allows an existing LLC or corporation to operate under a different brand name
  • Use a separate business name on marketing materials, invoices, contracts, and documents
  • Meet a state or local registration requirement for assumed or fictitious names
  • Establish a public record connecting a trade name with the underlying owner or entity

However: A DBA does not create a separate legal entity. If a sole proprietor registers a DBA, the underlying business is still generally a sole proprietorship. The DBA does not separate the owner's personal assets from the business's liabilities.

Who Typically Uses a DBA

  • Sole proprietors — A freelancer named Sarah Johnson might operate under the brand "Coastal Web Design" without forming an LLC
  • Existing LLCs — An LLC called Rivera Enterprises LLC might operate a landscaping business under the brand "Green Thumb Landscaping"
  • Corporations and other entities — May use an assumed or fictitious name where permitted by state law
  • Businesses with multiple brands — An owner may use one legal entity for several brands instead of creating a separate entity for every brand
Important: Registering a DBA does not give you nationwide trademark rights. The SBA distinguishes between entity names, DBAs, trademarks, and domain names because each serves a different purpose.

What Is an LLC (Limited Liability Company)?

An LLC is a legal business entity created under state law. Unlike a DBA, an LLC is an actual legal structure. It can own property, enter contracts, maintain its own business records, and incur liabilities separately from its owners.

What an LLC Can Provide

  • Limited liability protection under applicable state law
  • Separate legal identity from its owners
  • Flexible federal tax classification
  • A formal structure for ownership and management
  • The ability to operate under the LLC's legal name or a registered DBA
  • A framework for separating business and personal finances

For federal tax purposes, a single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity by default. A multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership. An eligible LLC may also elect S-corporation tax treatment. For a complete explanation, see our guide on how LLCs are taxed.

The Critical Distinction Most Guides Miss

A DBA is a name. An LLC is a legal entity. They are not two versions of the same business structure.

Simple Example:
Without an LLC: Sarah Johnson → DBA: Coastal Web Design — The DBA gives Sarah a business name, but does not create a separate legal entity.

With an LLC: Coastal Design LLC → DBA: Coastal Web Design — The LLC is the legal entity; the DBA is simply the operating or brand name.

Liability protection comes from the legal entity — not from the DBA registration. Think of an LLC as the legal structure and a DBA as the brand name. One LLC may be able to operate several brands using separate DBAs, depending on state and local rules.

DBA vs LLC Cost: What You Actually Pay

There is no single nationwide DBA or LLC price. DBA fees can be set by a state, county, city, or another local government. LLC formation fees are generally established by the state where the LLC is formed. For state-specific LLC costs, see our LLC cost guide.

  • DBA costs: Vary by jurisdiction; some jurisdictions require newspaper publication; renewal periods differ
  • LLC formation costs: Generally range from $35 to $500+ depending on the state; additional registered-agent, annual-report, and operating-agreement costs may apply

DBA vs LLC Taxes

A DBA does not change your federal tax classification. If you are a sole proprietor who registers a DBA, you are still taxed as a sole proprietor. An LLC has tax flexibility — a single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity by default, but an LLC can elect corporate or S-corp treatment.

Which One Do You Actually Need?

Consider a DBA Only If

  • You are an existing LLC or corporation and simply want to operate under another brand name
  • You want a business name without creating a new entity
  • Your state or local government requires a fictitious-name registration for your business name

Consider an LLC If

  • You want to separate your personal assets from business liabilities
  • Your business creates meaningful liability exposure
  • You want a formal legal entity
  • You expect employees, significant contracts, or business assets
  • You're considering different tax treatment in the future

Consider Both (LLC + DBA) If

  • You want the liability protection of an LLC AND want to operate under a different brand name
  • Your LLC name is formal/legal but you want a consumer-friendly brand name
  • You operate multiple brands under one LLC

Using an LLC + DBA Together

An LLC and a DBA are not mutually exclusive. Many businesses use both. For example, Maria owns Sunset Realty LLC (the legal entity). She also operates a property-management company and registers a DBA called "Golden Gate Property Management." Both names appear in her marketing, but the underlying legal entity is the same LLC.

Benefits of LLC + DBA combination: Liability protection through the LLC; flexible branding through the DBA; one legal entity can operate multiple brands; reduced formation cost compared to creating a new LLC for each brand. Note: The DBA does not create a separate liability shield for each brand. The underlying LLC and its liability protection apply to all DBA operations.

When to Move from DBA-Only to an LLC

Consider moving from a sole proprietorship with a DBA to an LLC when the business has meaningful liability exposure, revenue or contracts are growing, you're hiring employees, taking on significant business assets, or business partners are entering the picture.

The conversion is generally: Form the LLC → Get EIN → Open business bank account → Transfer business activity → Update DBA registration if needed. See our how to start an LLC guide for state-by-step instructions.

Frequently Asked Questions

01 What is the difference between a DBA and an LLC?

A DBA is a registered business name. An LLC is a legal business entity. A DBA does not create a separate legal entity or provide liability protection.

02 Do I need an LLC if I have a DBA?

Not necessarily. A DBA is simply a name registration. If you want liability protection and a separate legal entity, you need to form an LLC or another business entity.

03 Can an LLC have a DBA?

Yes. An LLC can register a DBA (doing business as) to operate under a different brand name. The LLC remains the legal entity behind the DBA.

04 Does a DBA provide liability protection?

No. A DBA is a name registration and does not create a separate legal entity or provide limited liability protection.

05 Is a DBA the same as an LLC?

No. A DBA is a registered business name. An LLC is a legal entity created under state law.

06 Can I have a DBA without an LLC?

Yes. A sole proprietor, partnership, existing LLC, or corporation can all register a DBA.

07 How much does a DBA cost compared to an LLC?

DBA costs vary by state, county, and city and are generally lower than LLC formation costs. However, DBA costs are jurisdiction-specific and should be verified with the local government. LLC formation fees can range from roughly $35 to $500+ depending on the state.

08 Does a DBA protect my business name?

A DBA registration provides limited name protection within the jurisdiction. It generally does not provide trademark protection.

09 Can I use both a DBA and an LLC?

Yes. Many businesses form an LLC and then register a DBA to operate under a different brand name. The LLC provides liability protection while the DBA provides branding flexibility.

10 Do I need to pay taxes differently with a DBA?

Not because of the DBA itself. A DBA does not change your tax classification. You are taxed based on your underlying business structure.

11 Can an LLC operate under multiple DBAs?

Yes, generally. A single LLC may be able to register multiple DBAs and operate multiple brands, depending on state and local rules.

12 Which is better for a small business, DBA or LLC?

For liability protection and a formal business entity, an LLC is generally more appropriate. A DBA is useful when you simply want to operate under a different name without creating a new entity.
