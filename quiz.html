The Four Business Structures Explained

Choosing the right legal structure is one of the first and most critical decisions every entrepreneur faces. The legal entity you choose determines your personal liability exposure, income tax obligations, fundraising capability, and ongoing administrative burden.

The core trade-off every business owner faces comes down to balancing simplicity vs. personal asset protection & tax savings. Below is a detailed breakdown of each structure.

1. Sole Proprietorship — Simple, but zero liability protection

A Sole Proprietorship is an unincorporated business owned and operated by one person. It is the default structure when you start doing business without registering a formal legal entity with your state.

⚠️ Key Characteristics & Risks:

  • Zero Personal Asset Protection: There is no legal distinction between you and your business. Business debts, lawsuits, or slip-and-fall claims can jeopardize your personal savings, home, and assets.
  • Pass-Through Taxing: Profits are reported directly on Schedule C of your personal Form 1040 tax return. All net profit is subject to 15.3% Self-Employment Tax.
  • Zero State Formation Fees: No Articles of Organization filing is required (though local business licenses or DBAs may still apply).

Best for: Low-risk, low-revenue side projects or freelancers testing a business concept before formally committing capital.

2. Limited Liability Company (LLC) — The sweet spot for most small businesses

A Limited Liability Company is a legal entity created under state law that combines the personal asset protection of a corporation with the tax simplicity and operational flexibility of a partnership.

✨ Key Features & Benefits:

  • Limited Liability Shield: Protects your personal assets (home, personal savings, vehicle) from business liabilities, lawsuits, and commercial debts.
  • Default Pass-Through Taxation: Avoids corporate double taxation. Profits pass directly to members' personal tax returns.
  • Flexible Tax Classification: An LLC can choose to be taxed as a Sole Prop/Disregarded Entity, Partnership, S-Corp, or C-Corp.
  • Minimal Corporate Formalities: Unlike corporations, LLCs are not strictly required to hold formal annual shareholder meetings or maintain board minutes.

Best for: 90%+ of small business owners, freelancers, e-commerce sellers, consultants, and real estate investors looking for strong asset protection without corporate bureaucracy.

3. S-Corporation (S-Corp Election) — Tax savings for profitable LLCs

An S-Corporation is not a separate legal business entity; it is a tax classification election made with the IRS (via Form 2553). An LLC or C-Corp can elect to be taxed as an S-Corp to reduce self-employment taxes once net profit becomes substantial.

TAX SAVINGS EXAMPLE

$100,000 Profit Tax Savings Example (LLC vs. S-Corp)

Standard Single-Member LLC

~$15,300 Tax

Entire $100k net profit subject to 15.3% Self-Employment Tax (Social Security & Medicare).

LLC Taxed as S-Corp

~$7,650 Tax

Pay yourself $50k reasonable salary (SE tax on $50k only). Take remaining $50k as dividend distribution (0% SE tax). Saves ~$7,650/yr!

The S-Corp requirements most people overlook

The S-Corp election comes with several restrictions worth understanding before you commit. Your LLC or corporation must have fewer than 100 shareholders, all shareholders must be U.S. citizens or permanent residents, and you cannot have more than one class of stock. Additionally, you must pay yourself a reasonable salary — which the IRS actively scrutinizes — and run payroll, file quarterly payroll tax returns, and submit Form 2553 to make the election. For this reason, most business owners who elect S-Corp status also hire a CPA specifically to manage the compliance.

Best for: Profitable LLCs making over $80,000 to $100,000 in net annual profit per owner.

4. C-Corporation (C-Corp) — Built for venture capital & growth startups

A C-Corporation is the structure of choice for venture-backed startups, companies seeking institutional investment, and businesses planning to eventually go public. It's also the most complex and expensive structure to form and maintain — which is precisely why it's the wrong choice for the vast majority of small business owners. Nevertheless, for the specific use cases it's designed for, no other structure comes close.

Why investors require C-Corps — and why everyone else should avoid them

Venture capital firms, angel investors, and most institutional investors specifically require C-Corp structure because it allows unlimited shareholders, multiple classes of stock (common and preferred), and clean cap table management. Additionally, C-Corps can take advantage of Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) treatment under Section 1202 of the tax code — which can mean a complete federal capital gains tax exclusion on up to $10 million in gains. These are powerful benefits, but they come with a significant downside: double taxation. The C-Corp pays corporate income tax on its profits, and then shareholders pay personal income tax again on any dividends. Consequently, unless you're specifically building for investment or an exit, a C-Corp is almost never the right choice for a small business owner.

ℹ️ Delaware C-Corp — why startups default to it: The majority of venture-backed startups incorporate as C-Corps in Delaware regardless of where they actually operate. Delaware has a highly developed body of corporate law, well-established legal precedents, and a Court of Chancery that handles corporate disputes without juries — which investors and attorneys favor. Furthermore, most standard VC term sheets and funding documents are written for Delaware C-Corps by default. If you're building something investor-backed, incorporating in Delaware is therefore typically the path of least resistance.

Best for: High-growth tech startups seeking VC investment, issuing employee stock equity, or planning an eventual IPO.

State-specific factors that change the math

The comparison above reflects federal-level tax treatment, but state taxes add another layer of complexity. California, for example, charges LLCs an $800 minimum franchise tax regardless of revenue — which makes a Sole Proprietorship genuinely more cost-effective in the early stages there. Texas charges no state income tax on LLCs with revenue under roughly $2.47 million, making it one of the most LLC-friendly states for small businesses. Additionally, some states — including New York, Pennsylvania, and California — impose their own S-Corp-level taxes or minimum fees that reduce the net savings from an S-Corp election. Therefore, state-specific factors should always be part of your structure decision, not just federal tax treatment.

How to Choose the Right Business Structure — A Decision Framework

If the quiz gave you a clear result, you likely don't need this section. However, if you're still uncertain — or if your situation bridges two structures — this decision framework gives you a systematic way to think through the choice.

Step 1 — Start with liability, not taxes

The most common mistake people make when choosing a business structure is leading with tax optimization. In reality, liability protection should be the first filter. If your business has any realistic exposure to lawsuits, contract disputes, employee issues, or physical premises — and most do — then a Sole Proprietorship is automatically off the table regardless of how simple or inexpensive it is. Once liability protection is established as a requirement, the remaining question is which protected structure (LLC, S-Corp, or C-Corp) is most appropriate.

Step 2 — Apply the revenue threshold test

1

Under $10,000 net profit annually

A Sole Proprietorship is acceptable if your liability exposure is genuinely low. Otherwise, form an LLC — the $50–$500 formation fee is worth the protection. The S-Corp election provides no meaningful tax savings at this revenue level and adds unnecessary complexity.

2

$10,000–$50,000 net profit annually

An LLC is almost always the right answer here. Pass-through taxation keeps things simple, liability protection covers you, and the S-Corp savings don't yet outweigh the added cost of payroll and extra accounting. Focus on growing revenue rather than optimizing taxes at this stage.

3

$50,000–$500,000 net profit annually

This is the S-Corp sweet spot. At $50,000 and above, the self-employment tax savings from an S-Corp election start to clearly exceed the payroll and accounting costs. Most LLC owners at this revenue level should consult a CPA specifically about making the S-Corp election — it's one of the highest-ROI tax planning moves available to small business owners.

4

Raising outside investment or building for an exit

If your goal is venture capital, angel investment, or eventually going public, a C-Corporation in Delaware is the standard choice. Alternatively, if you want to keep your options open without committing to a C-Corp now, forming an LLC first and converting later is also a viable path — though conversion does involve some legal complexity and cost.

Step 3 — Confirm with a CPA before you file

This quiz and guide provide solid general guidance — but your specific situation may have nuances that change the recommendation. Particularly if you're choosing between an LLC and an S-Corp election, or if you operate in multiple states, a one-hour consultation with a CPA is money extremely well spent. Most CPAs can walk you through the structure decision in under an hour for $150–$300. That investment is trivial compared to the cost of choosing the wrong structure and needing to restructure later.

Start as an LLC and upgrade later — a widely underused strategy

💡 You can make the S-Corp election after you've already formed your LLC: One of the most underused strategies for growing businesses is forming an LLC now — when simplicity matters most — and making the S-Corp tax election later when revenue justifies it. The election can typically be made at any time for the following tax year. This approach gives you liability protection and simplicity today, with a clear upgrade path when your income crosses the threshold where S-Corp savings become material.

Frequently Asked Questions — Business Structure

1 What is the best business structure for a small business?
For 90%+ of small business owners, a Limited Liability Company (LLC) is the best choice. It offers personal asset protection without the complex tax filings and mandatory corporate meetings required for C-Corporations.
2 What is the difference between an LLC and a Sole Proprietorship?
A Sole Proprietorship has zero legal separation between the owner and the business, meaning your personal bank accounts, home, and assets can be seized to pay business debts or lawsuit judgments. An LLC creates a separate legal entity that shields your personal assets.

S-Corp, C-Corp, and tax elections explained

3 When should I consider an S-Corp instead of an LLC?
An S-Corp election becomes advantageous once your LLC generates more than $50,000 to $80,000 in net annual profit per owner. By paying yourself a reasonable salary and taking the rest as distributions, you can save thousands in self-employment taxes.

C-Corp vs S-Corp and changing structures over time

4 What is the difference between an S-Corp and a C-Corp?
A C-Corp is subject to corporate double taxation (taxed at corporate level and again on individual dividends) but allows unlimited shareholders and VC funding. An S-Corp is a pass-through tax status limited to 100 U.S. shareholders.
5 Can I change my business structure later?
Yes! You can start as a Single-Member LLC, elect S-Corp status via IRS Form 2553 when profit grows, or convert to a Delaware C-Corp if you decide to raise institutional capital.

Costs, compliance, and practical next steps

6 How much does it cost to form each type of business structure?
Sole Proprietorships cost $0 to start. LLC state filing fees range from $35 (Montana) to $500 (Massachusetts). An S-Corp election is free with the IRS (Form 2553). C-Corps incur state filing fees plus higher ongoing legal and annual franchise tax fees.

Whether you need a lawyer — and which structure is easiest to maintain

7 Do I need a lawyer to choose and form my business structure?
Most entrepreneurs do not need a lawyer for a standard single-member or multi-member LLC. You can file directly with the state or use an online service like Northwest ($39) or ZenBusiness ($0). Legal counsel is recommended if you have complex equity splits or institutional investors.
8 What is the easiest business structure to maintain?
Sole Proprietorships require no ongoing state filings. For protected structures, an LLC is by far the easiest to maintain because it does not require formal annual shareholder meetings, board resolutions, or complex corporate minutes.
