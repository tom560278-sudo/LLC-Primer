The Four Business Structures Explained

Choosing a business structure is one of the first important decisions an entrepreneur faces. The structure you choose can affect personal liability, federal and state tax treatment, ownership and fundraising options, and ongoing administrative requirements.

The practical trade-off often involves balancing simplicity, liability protection, tax treatment, and administrative complexity. Below is a detailed breakdown of four common business structures.

1. Sole Proprietorship — Simple, but no separate liability shield

A Sole Proprietorship is an unincorporated business owned and operated by one person. It generally exists automatically when an individual conducts business without forming a separate legal entity, although state and local registration, licensing, or DBA requirements may still apply.

⚠️ Key Characteristics & Risks:

  • No Separate Liability Shield: The business and owner are not separate legal entities. Business debts and legal claims can therefore expose the owner's personal assets, subject to applicable law and any available protections.
  • Pass-Through Taxation: Business income and expenses are generally reported on the owner's individual federal tax return, commonly using Schedule C. Net earnings from self-employment are generally subject to Self-Employment Tax, which consists of Social Security and Medicare taxes. The exact amount depends on the owner's taxable earnings and applicable rules.
  • No State Entity Formation Filing: A sole proprietorship generally does not require Articles of Organization or similar LLC formation documents. However, business licenses, permits, assumed-name/DBA filings, and other registrations may still be required.

Best for: Some low-risk, owner-operated businesses, freelancers, or individuals testing a business concept who want a simple structure and understand the lack of a separate liability shield.

2. Limited Liability Company (LLC) — Liability protection with flexible tax treatment

A Limited Liability Company is a legal entity created under state law. An LLC generally provides a liability shield for its owners while offering flexible federal tax treatment and fewer formalities than a corporation.

✨ Key Features & Benefits:

  • Limited Liability Shield: An LLC generally separates the owner's personal assets from the company's business liabilities. The protection has important exceptions, including certain personal guarantees, personal wrongdoing, and situations where applicable legal requirements are not followed.
  • Default Pass-Through Taxation: A single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity for federal income-tax purposes unless it makes a different election. A multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership unless it elects otherwise.
  • Flexible Tax Classification: An LLC may generally elect to be taxed as a disregarded entity, partnership, S-Corporation, or C-Corporation when eligible and when the applicable tax election is made.
  • Fewer Corporate Formalities: LLCs generally have fewer mandatory corporate formalities than corporations, although state law and the LLC's operating agreement can establish additional requirements.

Best for: Many small-business owners who want a separate legal entity and liability protection while retaining flexibility in how the business is taxed and managed.

3. S-Corporation (S-Corp Election) — A Tax Classification With Additional Requirements

An S-Corporation is generally a federal tax status, rather than a separate state-law business entity. An eligible LLC or corporation can elect S-Corporation tax treatment with the IRS, generally by filing Form 2553.

For some profitable businesses, S-Corp taxation can reduce the portion of business earnings subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes because eligible owners who work in the business generally receive reasonable compensation as wages, while remaining eligible business income may be treated differently for employment-tax purposes.

The S-Corp requirements many owners overlook

S-Corp taxation comes with eligibility and compliance requirements. Generally, an S-Corporation may have no more than 100 shareholders, must meet specific shareholder eligibility rules, and generally may have only one class of stock. Certain trusts, estates, and tax-exempt organizations can also qualify as shareholders under specific rules.

An owner who works for the S-Corporation generally must receive reasonable compensation for services provided before taking non-wage distributions. The business must also handle payroll and employment-tax obligations and file the required federal and state returns.

For these reasons, the potential tax benefits should be weighed against the additional payroll, accounting, filing, and administrative requirements. A qualified tax professional can help determine whether an S-Corp election is appropriate for a particular business.

Best for: Some profitable businesses where the potential employment-tax treatment of S-Corp taxation may justify the additional compliance and administrative costs. There is no universal $80,000–$100,000 profit threshold at which an S-Corp election automatically becomes beneficial.

4. C-Corporation (C-Corp) — Common for Venture-Backed & High-Growth Companies

A C-Corporation is a separate legal entity that can be useful for companies seeking venture capital or institutional investment, issuing different classes of stock, building a larger ownership structure, or potentially pursuing a public offering. It can also be used by businesses that are not venture-backed, although its tax and compliance structure may be more involved than other options.

A C-Corporation generally permits unlimited shareholders and multiple classes of stock, subject to applicable corporate and securities rules. These characteristics can make the structure compatible with certain institutional investment and equity-compensation arrangements.

Why C-Corps Are Common in Venture-Backed Companies

Venture capital and institutional investors often invest through structures that are designed around corporate stock, preferred shares, option plans, and standardized financing documents. A C-Corporation can accommodate these arrangements more directly than many other business structures.

C-Corporations can also potentially qualify for Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) treatment under Section 1202 if the corporation and shareholder meet the applicable requirements. The potential federal gain exclusion can be substantial, but eligibility depends on factors including when the stock was acquired, the corporation's assets and activities, and the shareholder's holding period and circumstances. It should not be treated as an automatic benefit of forming a C-Corporation.

The primary federal tax consideration is that a C-Corporation is generally subject to corporate income tax on its taxable income, while shareholders may separately owe tax when the corporation distributes dividends. The actual tax result depends on the corporation's income, deductions, distributions, shareholder circumstances, and applicable federal and state taxes.

ℹ️ Delaware C-Corp — why startups often consider it: Delaware is widely used for venture-backed companies because of its established corporate statute, extensive body of corporate case law, and Court of Chancery. Investors, attorneys, and financing documents may be familiar with Delaware corporate structures. However, incorporating in Delaware does not eliminate the company's obligations in states where it actually conducts business; foreign qualification and additional state taxes or fees may apply.

Best for: Companies pursuing institutional or venture investment, businesses that need flexible equity structures, or companies planning for significant outside ownership or a potential public offering.

State-specific factors that change the math

Federal tax treatment is only part of the structure decision. State taxes, franchise taxes, annual reports, filing fees, payroll taxes, and other state-specific requirements can materially affect the cost of operating through a particular structure.

For example, California generally imposes an $800 annual franchise tax on LLCs subject to the tax, while corporations can be subject to different California tax rules. Texas does not impose a traditional individual or corporate income tax, but Texas businesses can still have franchise-tax and other state obligations depending on their circumstances.

S-Corp taxation can also have different state treatment. Some states recognize the federal S-Corp election differently, while others impose entity-level taxes, fees, or minimum taxes. State treatment should therefore be reviewed alongside federal tax consequences before making an election.

How to Choose the Right Business Structure — A Decision Framework

If the quiz gave you a clear result, you can use this section to review the factors behind that result. If your circumstances bridge two structures, consider the following questions rather than relying on a single revenue threshold.

Step 1 — Start with liability and business risk

Liability exposure is an important early consideration. A Sole Proprietorship does not create a separate legal entity or liability shield, while an LLC or corporation generally provides a separate legal entity and limited-liability protection subject to important exceptions.

Consider the nature of the business, contractual obligations, employees, customers, physical premises, professional activities, and other potential sources of liability. Insurance and appropriate business practices can also be important regardless of the entity selected.

Step 2 — Consider tax treatment and administrative costs

1

Lower or early-stage profits

A Sole Proprietorship or default-taxed LLC may be simpler for some early-stage businesses. The appropriate choice depends on liability exposure, state requirements, tax circumstances, and the owner's objectives.

2

Moderate or higher profits

As profits increase, an eligible LLC owner may want to evaluate whether S-Corp taxation could provide a tax benefit after considering reasonable compensation and additional payroll and accounting costs.

3

Established businesses

For an established business, compare the ongoing tax treatment, state fees, payroll requirements, ownership structure, administrative burden, and potential future financing needs. An existing LLC can sometimes elect S-Corp taxation without changing its state-law entity, while other changes may require additional legal or tax planning.

4

Raising outside investment or building for an exit

If you plan to raise venture capital or issue equity to multiple investors, the ownership and financing structure becomes particularly important. A C-Corporation can generally accommodate unlimited shareholders and multiple classes of stock, while an S-Corporation has significant shareholder and stock-class restrictions. If outside investment is not part of the plan, an LLC may provide sufficient flexibility without adopting a corporate structure solely because it is common among venture-backed companies.

Step 3 — Consider outside investment and ownership plans

If you plan to raise venture capital or issue equity to multiple investors, the ownership and financing structure becomes particularly important.

A C-Corporation can generally accommodate unlimited shareholders and multiple classes of stock, making it compatible with many institutional financing arrangements. An S-Corporation has significant shareholder and stock-class restrictions, which can make it less suitable for certain investment structures.

If outside investment is not part of the plan, an LLC may provide sufficient flexibility without adopting a corporate structure solely because it is common among venture-backed companies.

Step 4 — Consider the state where you actually operate

The state where an entity is formed is only one part of the analysis. If a business conducts enough activity in another state to require foreign qualification, it may have registration fees, reports, taxes, or other obligations there as well.

Before choosing a formation state, compare the formation cost, annual obligations, foreign-qualification requirements, tax treatment, and practical operating location.

Step 5 — Revisit the structure as the business changes

A business does not necessarily have to keep the same structure forever. Changes in profitability, ownership, employees, liability exposure, financing plans, or tax objectives can create reasons to reconsider the structure.

For example, an owner-operated business might begin as a sole proprietorship or LLC and later evaluate an S-Corp tax election. A company preparing for institutional investment might evaluate whether a C-Corporation better accommodates its planned financing and ownership structure.

The appropriate choice depends on the business's specific circumstances rather than a single revenue threshold or a universal “best” structure.

Step 3 — Confirm with a CPA before you file

This quiz and guide provide general educational information, but your specific circumstances may change which structure or tax election makes sense. This is particularly relevant when comparing an LLC with an S-Corp election, operating in multiple states, planning to add owners, or expecting significant changes in profitability.

A CPA or other qualified tax professional can help compare the expected tax treatment with the additional payroll, accounting, filing, and administrative requirements. Professional fees depend on the provider, location, scope of work, and complexity of your situation, so ask for the consultation fee before scheduling.

Start as an LLC and consider an S-Corp election later

💡 You can potentially make an S-Corp election after forming an LLC: An LLC can generally remain taxed under its default federal classification initially and later elect S-Corporation tax treatment if it meets the eligibility requirements. This can allow an owner to evaluate the business's profitability and tax circumstances before taking on the additional payroll and compliance requirements associated with S-Corp taxation.

S-Corp election deadlines and eligibility rules apply, and late-election relief may be available in certain circumstances. The election should therefore be planned with the applicable IRS deadlines and the business's tax year in mind rather than being treated as an automatic upgrade once revenue reaches a particular number.

Frequently Asked Questions — Business Structure

1 What is the best business structure for a small business?
There is no single business structure that is best for every small business. The appropriate choice depends on factors such as liability exposure, ownership, tax treatment, financing plans, state requirements, and administrative preferences.

A Sole Proprietorship is generally the simplest structure but does not create a separate legal entity or liability shield. An LLC can provide limited-liability protection while offering flexible federal tax classification. An S-Corp election may be worth evaluating for an eligible business when the potential employment-tax benefits justify the additional payroll and compliance requirements. A C-Corporation can be particularly relevant for companies seeking institutional investment or more complex equity structures.
2 What is the difference between an LLC and a Sole Proprietorship?
The primary legal difference is that an LLC is a separate entity created under state law, while a Sole Proprietorship does not create a separate business entity. A sole proprietor can therefore be personally liable for business debts and obligations, while an LLC generally provides its owners with limited-liability protection subject to applicable exceptions.

For federal tax purposes, a single-member LLC is generally treated as a disregarded entity unless it elects different tax treatment. An individual owner of a single-member LLC operating a trade or business is generally subject to self-employment tax in the same manner as a sole proprietor.

LLC formation fees are determined by the state and can vary substantially. A Sole Proprietorship generally does not require an entity-formation filing, although licenses, permits, and DBA requirements may still apply.

For a detailed comparison, see our LLC vs Sole Proprietorship guide.

S-Corp, C-Corp, and Tax Elections Explained

3 When should I consider an S-Corp instead of an LLC?
An S-Corp is generally a tax classification rather than a separate state-law business entity. An eligible LLC can elect S-Corporation treatment with the IRS by filing Form 2553.

There is no universal $50,000, $80,000, or $100,000 profit threshold at which an S-Corp election automatically becomes beneficial. The potential benefit depends on factors including the owner's reasonable compensation, business profit, payroll costs, state taxes, accounting costs, and the owner's overall tax situation.

An owner who performs services for an S-Corporation generally must receive reasonable compensation before taking non-wage distributions. The business also takes on additional payroll and tax-filing responsibilities.

For the full comparison, see our LLC vs S-Corp guide.

C-Corp vs S-Corp and Changing Structures Over Time

4 What is the difference between an S-Corp and a C-Corp?
The primary differences involve federal tax treatment, ownership restrictions, and equity structure.

A C-Corporation is generally subject to federal corporate income tax, and shareholders may also owe tax when the corporation distributes taxable dividends. The federal corporate income tax rate is generally 21%.

An S-Corporation generally passes its income, deductions, losses, and credits through to its shareholders for federal tax purposes. To qualify, an S-Corporation generally must have no more than 100 shareholders, only allowable shareholders, and one class of stock, among other requirements. Nonresident alien shareholders are not permitted.

These restrictions can make a C-Corporation more suitable for some businesses seeking institutional investment, multiple classes of stock, or broader ownership arrangements. They do not mean that every investor-backed business must use a C-Corporation.
5 Can I change my business structure later?
Yes. Businesses can change their legal structure or federal tax classification, although the process and consequences depend on the structures involved, state law, tax elections, ownership, and the timing of the change.

For example, an LLC can generally later elect S-Corporation tax treatment if it meets the applicable requirements. Form 2553 has specific filing deadlines, and late-election relief may be available in certain circumstances.

Other conversions, such as changing an LLC into a corporation or restructuring a C-Corporation, can involve additional legal, tax, filing, and ownership considerations. Professional advice can be useful when the change is significant.

Costs, Compliance, and Practical Next Steps

6 How much does it cost to form each type of business structure?
Costs depend heavily on the state and the structure being created.

A Sole Proprietorship generally does not have a separate entity-formation fee, although licenses, permits, DBA registrations, and other local requirements may apply.

An LLC requires a state formation filing in the state where it is created. The filing fee, recurring fees, and other state requirements differ by jurisdiction.

An S-Corp does not represent a separate state-law entity by itself. If an LLC elects S-Corp tax treatment, the business generally pays the underlying LLC formation and state compliance costs plus the additional costs associated with payroll, tax preparation, and S-Corp compliance. Form 2553 itself does not carry a federal filing fee.

A C-Corp has state formation and ongoing compliance costs that depend on the jurisdiction. Businesses incorporated in one state but operating in others may also have foreign-qualification obligations.

Whether You Need a Lawyer — and Which Structure Is Easiest to Maintain

7 Do I need a lawyer to choose and form my business structure?
Not necessarily. Many businesses can complete a basic Sole Proprietorship or LLC formation directly through the appropriate state filing system.

However, professional advice can be useful when the decision involves S-Corp taxation, multiple states, multiple owners, outside investment, complex ownership arrangements, intellectual property, regulated activities, or a conversion from one structure to another.

A CPA can help evaluate tax treatment, while an attorney can address legal structure, ownership, contracts, and other legal issues. Professional fees depend on the provider and scope of work, so confirm the cost before engaging a professional.
8 What is the easiest business structure to maintain?
A Sole Proprietorship generally has fewer entity-level formalities because there is no separate legal entity to maintain. An LLC generally requires more administration, including any state reports, taxes, registered-agent requirements, and other obligations applicable in its state.

S-Corp taxation adds payroll and additional federal and state tax-reporting requirements. C-Corporations generally involve more formal corporate governance, recordkeeping, ownership documentation, and reporting requirements than a Sole Proprietorship or many LLCs.
